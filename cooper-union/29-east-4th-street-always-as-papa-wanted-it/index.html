Every line traces one sealed house through a century of the same rooms — Joseph Brewster’s 1832 speculative row, Seabury Tredwell’s parlors with their secret chambers and dummy door, and Gertrude, who died in 1933 in the same canopied bed where she was born ninety-three years before. Old Merchant's House (Seabury Tredwell House)

Old Merchant's House

“Ionic column screen dividing parlors like a throne”

Old Merchant's House

"The interior, on the other hand, contains stylish Greek forms, notably the Ionic column screen that separates the front and rear parlors on the main floor." Postal & Dolkart, (2009) Guide to New York City Landmarks, p. 63.

08

“Fourteen-foot ceilings, egg-and-dart in the plaster”

Old Merchant's House, Formerly the Seabury-Tredwell House

"highly decorative plasterwork, such as a rosette in the entranceway and cornice with egg-and-dart moldings. The front and rear parlors are identical, with 14-foot ceilings, eight-panel mahogany doors." Spielvogel, (2016) The Landmarks of New York, p. 133.

09

“high-relief leaf disaster of beauty, off-white woodwork, ashlar sham marble master”

Old Merchant's House, Formerly the Seabury-Tredwell House

"the mahogany stairways are partially carved with acanthus leaves in high relief, and the woodwork here, as elsewhere in the house, is painted off-white... its simulated Siena marble walls." Spielvogel, (2016) The Landmarks of New York, p. 133.

10

“Two heavy wooden doors, one real and one a lie — a dummy that opens on blank brick, the wall's confession dry”

Merchant's House Museum

"the parlor was built with two heavy wooden doors-one to use, and a dummy that opens onto a blank brick wall, revealing the structure of the walls." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

11

“Secret chambers on both sides, so cleverly concealed a careful watcher misses them, sliding panels sealed, storing finery and wealth, a purpose unrevealed”

Merchant Prince

"Secret chambers, in the best mystery story tradition, were built in on both sides of the two parlors and are so cleverly camouflaged that even a careful observer would never know they existed. These secret rooms are large enough to accommodate several people but why they were built or how they were used is not known." Dunshee, (1952) As You Pass By, p. 219.

12

“a domed cistern-top the facade can't account for — four thousand gallons”

Merchant's House Museum

"Outside the kitchen was the garden, which held the four-thousand-gallon cistern that fed water into the kitchen and provided water for washing upstairs as well." Appelbaum & Halpern, (2013) Not the Met, p. 96.

Old Merchant's House

"The Old Merchant's House was built on speculation by Joseph Brewster as part of a row of six identical dwellings." Postal & Dolkart, (2009) Guide to New York City Landmarks, p. 63.

17

“Mayflower Brewster blood, speculation-built and paid”

Old Merchant's House

"Joseph Brewster, a descendane of Elder William Brewster of the Plymouth Colony, built it on what was originally part of the old Elbert Herring farm." Dunshee, (1952) As You Pass By, p. 219.

18

“the Elbert Herring farm the ground before the trade”

Old Merchant's House

"built it on what was originally part of the old Elbert Herring farm." Dunshee, (1952) As You Pass By, p. 219.

19

“pleasure gardens breathing where the Vauxhall used to talk”

Merchant's House Museum

"There is a charming little garden in back, which now looks out on brick walls and barbed wire, but once backed onto the Vauxhall Pleasure Garden." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

20

“backed onto the garden — bluestone path around the walk”

Merchant's House

"Many lovely shrubs and plants were recent plantings, although the bluestone path around the garden is original." Appelbaum & Halpern, (2013) Not the Met, p. 96.

21

“Then eighteen-thirty-five, Seabury Tredwell turned the lock, eighteen thousand dollars, hardware merchant on the climb”

Old Merchant's House

"New York City Ghost Stories tells us the House was bought in 1835 for $18,000 by Seabury Tredwell, a merchant on the way up." Lanigan-Schmidt, (2003) Ghosts of New York City, p. 99.

22

“five daughters, two sons, and Gertrude born in 'forty's time”

Old Merchant's House

"including five daughters and two sons. Five years later, his last daughter, Gertrude, was born." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

23

“second-floor front bedroom, canopied four-poster prime, ninety-three years in that same bed, she died in 'thirty-three's chime”

Merchant Prince

"She made her appearance in 1840 in the luxuriously canopied four-poster mahogany bed in the second floor front bedroom and died ninety-three years later in the same bed, in the same room." Dunshee, (1952) As You Pass By, p. 219.

24

“No closets in the walls — a room was taxed as room, so trunks held gloves and parasols, the bonnets and the plume, Chantilly lace and paisley shawls stored inside the gloom”

Merchant's House Museum

"there are no closets. Houses were taxed according to the number of rooms, and closets counted as rooms... In the house are trunks full of clothes, gowns, gloves, hats, Chantilly lace, paisley shawls." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

25

“Down the basement kitchen where the beehive oven runs, brick fireplace and Dutch oven, hand pump the cistern funds”

Merchant's House

"There, one can see the original cast-iron stove, the baking niche in a beehive oven, a pump for water, and a well-used wooden table." Appelbaum & Halpern, (2013) Not the Met, p. 96.

26

“the pie safe legs corroded in glasses set for one — vermin off the baked goods, and the little round tub done for bathing by the sink”

Merchant's House Museum

"Next to it stands the little round tub the family used for bathing. The legs on the pie safe are corroded where they were stood in glasses of water to keep vermin from the baked goods." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

27

“Morning-glory gas jets spun around the mirror's edge, twin flames beside the one who did her hair in danger — Mrs. Tredwell, redecoration begun in the eighteen-sixties, six daughters to marry, only two ever won”

Merchant's House Museum

"Mrs. Tredwell's mirror is lit by gas jets shaped to look like morning glories twining around the edge... Mrs. Tredwell redecorated in the 1860's to show the family's prosperity-she was trying to marry off six daughters. (Only two ever married.)" Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

28

“Kept it as Papa wanted it, shutters sealed from the light, felt stuffed in the windows, felt against the cold and night”

Merchant's House

"The windows were kept shuttered. Even minuscule bits of light were hidden with felt or other cloth to prevent light and cold from penetrating their inner sanctum." Lanigan-Schmidt, (2003) Ghosts of New York City, p. 99.

29

“the row of six torn down around it, out of sight, Brewster's twins all vanished, only this one left to fight”

Old Merchant's House

"This three-story brick town house completed in 1832 was one in a row of six. Joseph Brewster built the houses and lived in No. 29 until it was sold to Seabury Tredwell in 1835." McDarrah & McDarrah, (1990) Museums in New York, p. 309.

31

“disintegrating slow on a grubby street of stain”

Seabury Treadwell House

"as Ada Louise Huxtable observed in 1972, 'slowly disintegrating on a casually grubby street.'" Stern, (1995) New York 1960, p. 1092.

32

“Joseph Roberto relieved the bearing walls of their grief, seventy-four the interior landmarked past belief, seventy-nine restored”

Seabury Treadwell House

"Funds raised from a variety of public and private sources were used to conduct a much-needed structural renovation in 1972, supervised by New York University architect Joseph Roberto... The inside of the house... was designated a landmark in 1974 and underwent a restoration, also supervised by Roberto and completed in 1979." Stern, (1995) New York 1960, p. 1092.

33

“reproduction moquette relief, the French carpet Pompeii-patterned, mosaic underneath”

Old Merchant's House, Formerly the Seabury-Tredwell House

"carpeted from wall to wall with a reproduction French moquette carpet, whose pattern imitated the mosaic floors discovered at Pompeii and Herculaneum." Spielvogel, (2016) The Landmarks of New York, p. 133.

34

“red damask bed curtains too decayed to display — but bolts of the original still in the attic lay”

Merchant's House Museum

"After Gertrude's death, the magnificent red damask bed curtains were found to be too decayed to salvage, but there was no need to try a modern recreation; bolts of the original fabric were still stored in the attic." Kahn, (2002) City Secrets New York City, p. 125.

35

“and two-thousand-fourteen, the Commission gave the say to an eight-story hotel rising next door in the fray — excavation pressing on the party wall's gray, the fastidious survivor bracing for the sway”

Old Merchant's House, Formerly the Seabury-Tredwell House

"In April 2014, the Landmarks Preservation Commission approved the proposed construction of an eight-story hotel adjacent to the Museum, which is now working to ensure that the fastidiously preserved building will not be affected by excavation and construction on the adjacent site."
