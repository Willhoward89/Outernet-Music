“Brick or stone or wood, long debated, no one could tell, on a knoll just east of Third, near Eleventh or Twelfth it fell”

Governor Stuyvesant's mansion

"Whether the main body of the house was of brick or stone or wood has long been a matter of debate, as has also its exact location. Signs seem to point to its having been on a knoll just east of the present Third Avenue, somewhere about Eleventh or Twelfth Street." Harlow. (1931) The Old Bowery Days, p. 14.

14

“Dutch fashion flower beds in formal pattern out in front, an orchard to the south, a pear tree bearing the brunt”

Governor Stuyvesant's mansion

"The ground in front of it was very pleasantly laid out in Dutch fashion with flower beds in formal patterns. That an orchard adjoined it on the south is proven by the fact that one of the pear trees survived until well along in the nineteenth century." Harlow. (1931) The Old Bowery Days, p. 14.

“that famous slip from Holland, planted sixteen-sixty near, stood near two hundred years, till eighteen-sixty-seven's gale severe”

Stuyvesant's Bouwerie

"Near here he planted his famous pear tree from a slip brought home on his last visit to Holland. This stood for nearly two hundred years and bore fruit until February, 1867, when it was blown down in a fierce gale." Callender. (1929) Yesterday in Little Old New York, p. 56.

Stuyvesant's Bouwerie
