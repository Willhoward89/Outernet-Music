Genesis: From Alluvial Hillock to Civic Commons

Beneath the ordered geometry of the modern square lies a topography of pure accident. In its primordial state, (Union Square was originally a sand hill. Had it not been for the sand, Irving Place & Lexington Ave. would likely be part of Broadway today!) — a geological caprice that, in redirecting the city's northward march, quietly authored the very street grid we now take for granted. The nomenclature itself encodes a cartographic confluence, for (Union Square took its name from the fact that it was the spot where the Bowery and Bloomingdale Road (today's Broadway) came together, that is, united.) Situated at this juncture of thoroughfares, (The square marks the union of Fourth Avenue and Broadway and was saved from further development by the city in 1811.)

The parcel's provenance is a chronicle of colonial-era conveyances. (The land was once part of Elias Brevoort's farm, but in 1762 he sold twenty acres lying west of the Bowery Road, or Third Avenue, to John Smith, whose heirs subsequently sold it to Henry Spingler for $4750.) Municipal foresight then intervened, though with characteristic bureaucratic deliberation: (In 1807 Broadway was regulated from Astor Place to the vicinity of Twenty-third Street, and in 1815 the city designated part of the land that became Union Square as a public park. The city, however, did not take title to Union Square or lay out the land as a park until 1831.)

The rationale advanced for the acquisition betrays a shrewd anticipation of the speculative pressures to come. Municipal authorities reasoned that (now that the ground is unoccupied, the purchase can be effected, which, in the lapse of a few years, when valuable buildings are erected, will be impracticable, on account of the great expense.) Undergirding this fiscal prudence was a nascent theory of urban salubrity, the conviction that open ground furnished indispensable (ventilators to a densely populated city.) Thus was the commons conceived less as ornament than as civic respiratory apparatus.

The square's ascendancy to fashionable pre-eminence was foretold almost from its inception. (With its enviable location at what was then the northernmost limit of Broadway and just half a mile north of stylish Bond Street, Union Square was assured of a fashionable destiny, predicted the Herald as early as 1836.) The subsequent building boom proceeded at a velocity that astonished contemporaries, who marveled that (Whole streets of magnificent dwelling houses have been erected in the vicinity of Union Square within the last year.) The pivotal act of speculative release came when (Palatial row houses and mansions began to rise, facing Union Square itself, after the Bank of Manhattan sold the lots it owned around the perimeter of the park in 1845.) As mansions proliferated, the park was refined to complement its patrician setting, and (the park was planted with lawns, flower beds, trees, and shrubbery. Gas street lights were installed by 1849.)

The Belle Époque of the Square: Salon Culture and Genteel Repose

In its meridian, the square constituted the acknowledged locus of metropolitan gentility. From its opening in 1831, (Union Square quickly became the central gathering place for those who lived in the mansions nearby. Concert halls and artist societies further enhanced the cultured atmosphere, and high-end shopping quickly proliferated along Broadway, which was dubbed 'Ladies' Mile.') So thoroughgoing was the exclusion of commerce from these precincts that a visitor could remark, of the residential streets to the north, that (Not a store is to be seen...nothing but the dwellings of the aristocracy, who moved up here out of the noisy and dusty part of the city.)

The neighborhood's mercantile ethos insinuated itself even into the park's most whimsical furnishings. The avian dwellings suspended in the trees were (ingeniously contrived miniature buildings for these little birds, placed among the branches of the trees), and these confections were fashioned to (represent different business departments as 'The Post Office,' 'The Custom House,' 'The Exchange,' etc.) So relentlessly commercial was the surrounding sensibility that wags observed how (even the neighborhood birds had to be busy at work all day long) — a droll allegory of a moneyed class incapable of imagining leisure untethered from enterprise.

The Great Reversal: Trade Ascendant and the Patrician Exodus

The idyll proved evanescent. What (shocked and upset the gentility in the 1860s was the influx of shops into Union Square. They took over the basements and parlor floors of mansions and row houses, some of which were less than ten years old, and none of them more than twenty years old.) The tempo of transformation was such that, by mid-decade, (the Times reported that nearly all the houses on Union Square had become boardinghouses.)

Commerce advanced with an almost tidal inexorability, and (the blocks surrounding the formerly tranquil square hummed with commercial activity, with once-grand mansions converted to boardinghouses, stores, and offices rented to chiropodists, coal dealers, dressmakers, milliners, hairdressers, furriers, and cloak manufacturers.) The symbolic coup de grâce arrived when (IN 1870, TIFFANY'S DEMOLISHED the James Renwick-designed Church of the Puritans on the west side of Union Square and replaced it with a huge cast-iron store-the "Palace of Jewels.") — the supplanting of a sanctuary by a temple of luxury retail furnishing an almost too-perfect emblem of the age's transvaluation.

To dignify the increasingly commercial precinct, the municipality summoned the era's foremost landscape practitioners: (In 1872 the city hired Calvert Vaux and Frederick Law Olmsted, designers of Central Park, to remake Union Square according to a more up-to-date, picturesque aesthetic.) Meanwhile, spiraling valuations accelerated the flight of the old families, for by (1866 and 1867, houses on Union Square were selling for $125,000 to $180,000, up from the $15,000 that lots in the area had fetched ten or fifteen years earlier.) — a twelvefold appreciation that rendered residential occupancy an act of economic irrationality.

The Theater of the Republic: Rally, Rite, and Remonstrance

As its domestic character dissolved, the square assumed a grander vocation as the amphitheater of the body politic. In the crucible of civil conflict, (Union Square would replace City Hall Park as the center of civic life.) The most celebrated of its convocations unfolded in 1861, when (what was reputedly the largest assemblage ever seen on the continent—somewhere between 100,000 and 250,000 people—flooded into Union Square.) On that consecrated occasion, (The new bronze statue of Washington was wrapped in the flag that had been fired upon at Sumter) — a gesture in which the relic of a besieged fortress and the effigy of the republic's founder were fused into a single patriotic tableau.

The square's identity as a forum of dissent proved durable across generations. With the outbreak of hostilities, (the vast public space (large by New York standards, of course) was center stage for protesters of all sorts-from union workers to political activists.) In the ensuing decades it became a magnet for the era's insurgent movements, drawing (politically and socially driven organizations like the American Civil Liberties Union, the Communist and Socialist Parties, and the Ladies' Garment Workers Union to move in.) By the interwar years its radical complexion was unmistakable, such that (The Communists of New York make their headquarters at Union Square also.)

33 Union Square West — The Decker Building: Ornamental Gothic to Avant-Garde Atelier

At the square's northern flank stood an edifice whose successive incarnations trace the arc of the city's cultural history. Erected to house celebrated piano warerooms, it drew rhapsodic contemporary notice: (But, of all our new buildings., the handsomest Is the one on Union Square, for Decker Brothers' piano warerooms. This building, though not large, is in a new direction in street-architecture, and we may well hope to sec its example followed. It is of Ohio freestone and brick, in the style known as the ornamental Gothic, and, with the contrasts of tints in the material and its quaint windows, makes a most picturesque effect.)

The chronicler's enthusiasm swelled into a manifesto against the monotony of the prevailing idiom: (One is so weary of brownstone, sandstone, white marble, and painted iron, in our architecture, that a front like this, so full of light and shadow, so mellow and rich in tone, so fresh and pleasing in design, is a very agreeable revelation. What a superb quarter of the city Union Square would be, if fronted with buildings as inspiriting as this!) The instruments crafted within secured the firm an international eminence, for (The firm is one of the most prominent of representative piano-forte makers in the world, having won this proud position by the intrinsic merits of the instruments of its make.)

In the fullness of time, the building was inducted into the annals of the postwar avant-garde. (In 1968 Warhol moved downtown to the Decker Building, where he set up his White Factory. A stark contrast to the anything-goes vibe of the Silver Factory, Warhol's Union Square studio was set up like an office. It was here that he was shot by Valerie Solanas in July 1968.) As a corollary account specifies, the artist's coterie migrated in early 1968 to (the sixth floor of a ca. 1900 loft building at 33 Union Square West.) By the close of the century, its origins as a musical enterprise survived only as elegiac memory: (Only the Decker Piano Building remains (33 Union Square West). A beautiful architectural example of bygone times, its days as a piano firm are long gone, too.) The survey record consecrates its exotic idiom — a Moorish, Venetian composition wrought in (Brick, Limestone, Terra Cotta) and surmounted by a (Tile Roof) studded with (Islamic Star Roundels). The WPA corroborated this Orientalist flourish, noting that (at No. 33 the Union Building, built in 1893, has richly framed windows inspired by Spanish Moorish design.)

31 Union Square West — The Bank of the Metropolis: Tripartite Rigor in Limestone

Adjacent stood a paradigm of financial architecture's studied gravitas. (The familiar, sober classical vocabulary of bank architecture is enlivened here by a welcoming two-story bowed portico supported by a pair of gray granite Ionic columns facing Union Square.) Nor was its longer elevation neglected, for (The long 16th Street elevation also features some distinctive touches.)

The institution was woven into the commercial fabric of the district from its founding, having been (founded m 1871 to serve the needs of businesses in the Union Square area, maintained its offices on the square until 1918. when it was absorbed by the Bank of Manhattan.) Its architect commanded particular distinction as (an important early skyscraper designer with a particular interest in adapting the tripartite form of the column Io tad buildings) — a preoccupation legible in the building's disciplined base-shaft-capital articulation. Constrained by its lot, the structure presents a face of exceptional slenderness, for (Its Union Square front is extremely narrow.) The survey assigns its base the tone Coral Reef and its façade Pearl White, a marriage of (Rusticated Limestone, Granite Bulkheads, Polished Granite Columns) ascending to a (Limestone) shaft. The site had long served commerce, an 1885 atlas recording that at this address (there is written 'White Manfg.Co.')

33 East 17th Street — The Century Building: Queen Anne Erudition in Brick and Bluestone

At the square's upper margin presides a redbrick monument to the golden age of American periodical publishing. (The red brick. Queen Anne-style structure is one of the finest examples of the style and was built to house the many publishing activities of the Century Company.) In an alternative appraisal it emerges as a (red-brick-and-limestone Queen Anne pile comes straight out of Victorian London), a transatlantic import so faithful that it ranks among (one of the few extant major Queen Anne buildings in Manhattan.)

The firm it sheltered commanded a distinguished catalogue, for (Among its imprints were the Century and St. Nicholas magazines and a host of fine books.) In the vernal flower markets that once animated the square, its bulk dominated the horizon, since (The large building in the background is the Century Company Building Completed in 1881 on the north end of the square, it was first leased to the publishers of the popular Century and St Nicholas magazines.) Its architectural significance was ratified by its inclusion among the landmarked ensemble as (the Century Building (William Schickel, 1881), 33 East Seventeenth Street.) The survey records its base as Crystal and its façade as Brick Red, composed of (Red Brick, Wyoming Bluestone) at grade and (Brick, Terra Cotta, Wyoming Bluestone, Iron) above, crowned by a (Gambrel Roof) and ornamented with (Sunflower Terra Cotta Finials) — an Aesthetic Movement conceit rendered in fired clay.

45 East 17th Street — The Everett Building: Classical Revival on Hallowed Ground

Upon a site once graced by a celebrated hostelry rose a Beaux-Arts-inflected office block whose preservation reflected a broader vigilance. (Concerns about the fate of historic buildings on other sites around the square led to the designation as landmarks of...the Everett Building (Starrett & Van Vleck, 1908), 45 East Seventeenth Street.) The survey fixes its base at Antique Brass and its façade at Beige, executed in (Terra Cotta, Marble) at the base and (Terra Cotta, Polychrome Terra Cotta) across the elevation, in an idiom identified as (Classical-Revival.) The block bore an antecedent of renown, for (The Everett House, completed in 1854, stood at 41 East Seventeenth Street, on the north side of the park); and, as a later observer recorded, (The five-story building at the northwest corner of East 17th Street is the Everett House. Built in 1854, this hotel was convenient to 14th Street's shopping, musical and theatrical attractions.)

1-3 Union Square West — The Lincoln Building: Romanesque Gravitas

Presiding over a further corner stood a Romanesque revival veteran. (The Lincoln Building, erected at I Union Square in 1889, is an example adapted from Romanesque work.) It too was accorded protection, enumerated among the landmarks as (the Lincoln Building (R. H. Robertson, 1890), 1-3 Union Square West.)

100-102 East 17th Street — Tammany Hall: The Iconography of Legitimacy

At the southeastern corner of 17th Street rose an edifice conceived as a rhetorical instrument of political rehabilitation. (On the southeast corner of East 17th Street and Union Square East, at 100-102 East 17th Street, stands the second of the district's institutional buildings. Tammany Hall, once the headquarters of New York City's Democratic Earn political machine.) Its architectural vocabulary was deliberately declarative, presenting (a dignified architectural treatment, one of the chief motifs of which arc the severe Colonial columns in the centers of the Union Square and the Seventeenth-street facades which recall the days of early American architecture.)

The stylistic choice was freighted with intention, for (The Colonial Revival was undoubtedly chosen not only because it was reminiscent of the architecture of the early republic, bur also because it symbolized the government established during that period.) In effect, the organization pursued architectural absolution, since (The Society of Tammany was attempting to associate itself with the ideals of early American democracy.) Its material vocabulary reinforced the historicist argument: (This distinguished neo-Georgian facade, with its rusticated stone base, double-height pilasters, and sculptural reliefs in terra cotta and limestone, was inspired by the original Federal Hall.) The gambit was audacious given the institution's reputation, for (Tammany Hall, now a byword for municipal corruption, once dominated the City's political arena) — a machine that sought, through the borrowed dignity of Federal-era motifs, to launder its notoriety in the currency of republican virtue.

Coda: The Square as Architectural Palimpsest

To traverse the northern crown of the square is to read a stratified text in masonry, each layer bearing witness to a distinct chapter of the city's self-invention. (The diverse architecture of the buildings surrounding Union Square does not supply the unified feeling of enclosure implied by the word ''square,'' but it does offer an interesting record of architectural styles that have been popular in past years.) The commercial palaces share, for all their stylistic disparity, a governing compositional logic, whereby (Three divisions of each facade are clearly marked: a base ornamented with classical details, an intermediate portion of undecorated masonry pierced by regular windows, and a crowning element at the top consisting of arched windows and an elaborate cornice.)

The square endures as a repository of improbable heterogeneity, a place (like the Noah's Ark of New York, rescuing at least two of every kind from the curling seas of concrete.) For the discerning peripatetic, the counsel remains as apt as ever: (The trick to any stroll through this zone is to look up. Each building brings an animated story to the streetscape, and many have been lovingly restored and given meaningful, modern uses.)
