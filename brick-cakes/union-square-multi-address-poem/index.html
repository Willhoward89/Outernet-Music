Where the Roads Were Wed An Elegy in Stone

One. The Sleeping Dune.

Before the marble, before the mansions rose in pride, there was only wind and hush where the grains of sand did hide, for once, they say, (Union Square was originally a sand hill), and had the wind but turned, so goes the tale we tell until, (Irving Place and Lexington Avenue would likely be part of Broadway today).

Two roads walked out to meet the dusk and would not part, (the spot where the Bowery and Bloomingdale Road, today's Broadway, came together, that is, united) at the heart, so here the city keeps the vow no year has yet outgrown, this wedded ground that (marks the union of Fourth Avenue and Broadway) as its own.

A farmer's quiet held it first before the town was grown, (the land was once part of Elias Brevoort's farm), a field of seed and stone, and slow, so slow, the city swore to (lay out the land as a park), though it lingered, lingered, (until eighteen thirty-one) to leave its mark.

They took it bare beneath the sky before the price could climb, (now that the ground is unoccupied, the purchase can be effected) in good time, lest the coming years should make the bargain (impracticable) and cruel, and gave the choking town its breath, those tender (ventilators to a densely populated city) as its jewel.

Two. The Gilded Hush.

And prophecy proved kind, the green was (assured of a fashionable destiny) and grand, and one by one, like slow hosannas, (palatial row houses and mansions began to rise) across the land. They dressed the earth in finery, in (lawns, flower beds, trees, and shrubbery) at play, and lit the dark with early flame, for (gas street lights were installed by eighteen forty-nine) to light the way.

It (became the central gathering place for those who lived in the mansions nearby) and dear, where (not a store is to be seen), and only (the dwellings of the aristocracy) drew near.

Even the birds kept ledgers where the summer leaves would fall, in (ingeniously contrived miniature buildings, placed among the branches of the trees), so small, each carved to (represent different business departments, such as The Post Office, The Custom House, The Exchange) as well, until the whole green grove was bound by some commercial spell, and (even the neighborhood birds had to be busy at work all day long) in every call.

Three. When Gold Became Weather.

But splendor is a weather, not a wall that long will stand, for what (shocked and upset the gentility in the eighteen sixties was the influx of shops) across the land, into rooms still young and fine, (less than ten years old) and small, until (nearly all the houses on Union Square had become boarding houses) after all.

The parlors filled with strangers as the quiet turned to cost, and the air (hummed with commercial activity), the peace forever lost, with (chiropodists, coal dealers, dressmakers, milliners, hairdressers, furriers, and cloak manufacturers) in their throng, a temple torn to raise a glittering wrong. (Tiffany's demolished the James Renwick-designed Church of the Puritans) upon the day, and set instead (a huge cast-iron store, the Palace of Jewels) to gleam and stay.

Then, grieving what it broke, the city called its makers of the fair, (the city hired Calvert Vaux and Frederick Law Olmsted) to tend the wounded square, to (remake Union Square according to a more up-to-date, picturesque aesthetic) with their art, as if a broken thing replanted might regrow a beating heart.

Four. The People's Amphitheater.

And when the Union tore, the square became its wound and widening heart of stone, (Union Square would replace City Hall Park as the center of civic life) and stood alone. There rose a sea no shore could hold beneath the burning sun, (the largest assemblage ever seen on the continent, somewhere between one hundred thousand and two hundred fifty thousand people) as one, and over them, like some tall saint enrobed in living flame, (the new bronze statue of Washington was wrapped in the flag that had been fired upon at Sumter) in its name.

Forever after it became the throat of every cry and call, (center stage for protesters of all sorts), a hearth that welcomed all, that gathered in (the American Civil Liberties Union, the Communist and Socialist Parties, and the Ladies' Garment Workers Union) to be free, until (the Communists of New York make their headquarters) beneath the sheltering tree.

Five. The Decker Building.

Of all the faces round the green a single man would praise the best, (the handsomest is the one on Union Square, for Decker Brothers' piano warerooms) above the rest, a hymn of (Ohio freestone and brick, in the style known as the ornamental Gothic) fair and old, a warmer song than all the ordinary stone that left him cold.

For he was tired of the sameness that the plainer builders wrought, (so weary of brownstone, sandstone, white marble, and painted iron) his eye had sought, till here at last his weary sight found (a very agreeable revelation) new, and up he cried in joy, and swore his love was true, (what a superb quarter of the city Union Square would be, if fronted with buildings as inspiriting as this) come through.

Within, the hammers made a music heard from shore to distant shore, (one of the most prominent of representative piano-forte makers in the world) and more. And later came a paler ghost to haunt the upper floor, (in nineteen sixty-eight, Warhol moved downtown to the Decker Building, where he set up his White Factory) once more, and in that white and watching room, the story turned to gun, (he was shot by Valerie Solanas in July of nineteen sixty-eight) when day was done.

Now all the song is memory that lingers in the mortar's core, (only the Decker Piano Building remains) upon the old and quiet shore, its (days as a piano firm are long gone, too), yet still it seems to gleam, its skin a slow (brick, limestone, and terra cotta) Moorish dream, its brow a (tile roof) sown with (Islamic star roundels) up on high, where evening kneels within the (windows inspired by Spanish Moorish design) and the sky.

Six. The Bank of the Metropolis.

Beside it stands a psalm of quieter and steadier law, where (the familiar, sober classical vocabulary of bank architecture is enlivened) without flaw by (a welcoming two-story bowed portico supported by a pair of gray granite Ionic columns) at the door. It was (founded in eighteen seventy-one to serve the needs of businesses in the Union Square area) of yore, and reaching for the heavens, kept its slender, modest grace, (its Union Square front is extremely narrow) in that crowded place, a body cut from (rusticated limestone, granite bulkheads, and polished granite columns) deep and old, raised where commerce always slept, where (there is written, White Manufacturing Company) in the fold.

Seven. The Century Building.

Northward, a blossoming of brick and printed word, (the red brick, Queen Anne-style structure), the fairest ever heard, a (pile) that (comes straight out of Victorian London), proud and stern, one of (the few extant major Queen Anne buildings in Manhattan) at the turn. Within, the presses dreamed the sleeping age awake, their (imprints, the Century and Saint Nicholas magazines, and a host of fine books) for the reader's sake. And in the flower-markets of a spring now long since fled, it stood, (the large building in the background), rising overhead, a body of (red brick and Wyoming bluestone) sound and true, its brow a (gambrel roof) against the blue, its crown alight with (sunflower terra cotta finials) in the dew.

Eight. The Everett Building.

On ground once hallowed by the weary players' rest, where (the Everett House, completed in eighteen fifty-four) once held its lamp the best, so (convenient to Fourteenth Street's shopping, musical, and theatrical attractions) and its grace, there rose a later, cooler beauty in its place, (the Everett Building, by Starrett and Van Vleck, in nineteen oh eight) stood tall, a face of (terra cotta, and polychrome terra cotta) over all, that speaks the still and clear-eyed tongue of (Classical Revival) to us all.

Nine. The Lincoln Building.

And on its corner, grave with all the weathered years it wore, (the Lincoln Building, erected at One Union Square in eighteen eighty-nine) and more, an old and weathered soul that came (adapted from Romanesque work) of yore.

Ten. Tammany Hall.

At the southeast angle stood a hall in borrowed light, (Tammany Hall, once the headquarters of New York City's Democratic political machine) and might, that wore its columns like a stolen robe of saints upon the stone, (the severe Colonial columns, which recall the days of early American architecture) of its own, chosen since such stone (symbolized the government established during that period) of the free, a house forever (attempting to associate itself with the ideals of early American democracy) to be, its (neo-Georgian façade, inspired by the original Federal Hall) so grand, and yet, and yet, the name became (a byword for municipal corruption) in the land.

Eleven. Look Up.

So walk this crown, and read the stone that will not be one single thing, (the diverse architecture does not supply the unified feeling of enclosure implied by the word square) to sing, yet leaves (an interesting record of architectural styles that have been popular in past years) to bring. Each face still lifts its threefold prayer up to the open air, (a base ornamented with classical details) below, so fair, a middle (pierced by regular windows) rising there, a crown of (arched windows and an elaborate cornice) everywhere.

It is a strange surviving ark, this weathered, wondrous place, (like the Noah's Ark of New York, rescuing at least two of every kind from the curling seas of concrete) in its grace. And if you'd hear the whole slow story sung and lifted up, then raise your eyes, for, (the trick to any stroll through this zone is to look up).
