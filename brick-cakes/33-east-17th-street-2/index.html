I. Prefatory Statement on the Subject Structure

Few edifices in the northern precincts of Union Square repay sustained connoisseurial scrutiny so richly as the palimpsestic pile that presides over the through-block parcel carrying it from (33 East 17th Street) to its ancillary rear frontage upon (East 18th Street). Conceived at the confluence of mercantile ambition and aesthetic daring, the structure historically consecrated as the Century Building endures as among the most consequential and least commonplace survivals of Queen Anne commercial architecture that the borough of Manhattan yet retains. It (Was built in 1881.) to the designs of the architect (William Schickel), and submitted, in the twilight of the twentieth century, to a comprehensive campaign of rehabilitation dated (1994-95). That posterity now apprehends it as (BARNES & NOBLE, FORMERLY CENTURY BUILDING), disposed across (33 EAST 17TH STREET AND 38-46 EAST 18TH STREET, MANHATTAN), articulates at a stroke the dialectic of ceremonial front and utilitarian rear that governs its plan. It rises, in the topographical shorthand of the guides, at (33 East 17th St., bet. Park Ave. S. and Broadway to W. 18th St.), addressing the plaza (between Broadway and Fourth Avenue), so that this (red-brick-and-limestone Queen Anle pile comes straight out of Victorian London). What follows is an exhaustive anatomization of every ascertainable facet of its fabric, ornament, and materiality, marshalled wholly from the documentary sediment of the surviving record.

II. Stylistic Genealogy and the Aesthetic Movement Context

The building avows, without equivocation, its fealty to the Queen Anne idiom, its manner set down with lapidary economy as (Queen-Anne, neo-Grec: Secondary Façade 18th Street) — a formulation that at once consecrates the ceremonial elevation to the picturesque revivalist temper while consigning the rear to the more astringent, incised classicism of the neo-Grec. The mode is one (distinguished by its picturesque mixture of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English motifs), a studied heterogeneity within which, (Although classical details are commonly used (such as the Jacobean doorway), these are combined in an unusual and thoroughly nonclassical way.) The manner did not spring unbidden from the ground but issued from a broader cultural efflorescence, having been (Championed by such artists and writers as James McNeill Whistler and Oscar Wilde, the Queen Anne style developed along with the English Aesthetic movement.)

The singular distinction of the choice resides in its very anomaly, for (The Century Building is one of the few surviving commercial examples of the Queen Anne style in the city); it numbers among (one of the few extant major Queen Anne buildings in Manhattan); and, categorically, (Queen Anne-style commercial buildings are rare in NYC.) As the connoisseur's compendia affirm, (The red brick Century Building is a rare New York example of a Queen Anne- style commercial structure.) The idiom being overwhelmingly the province of the domestic and the suburban, its migration onto a speculative commercial block bespeaks a genuine act of architectural audacity, for (William Schickel might have used the Queen Anne style, which was common in domestic suburban designs but almost unknown in urban commercial structures, to draw attention to what was essentially a speculative office building.) The stratagem was born of a quarter then in the full flood of its ascendancy: (The Union Square area was booming in the last quarter of the nineteenth century. Unusual or distinctive building design would have been one way of appealing to potential tenants, especially those who thought themselves socially or artistically progressive.) For the discernment of its rare stylistic kindred, one need only invoke the (Gramercy apartments (1883. G. W da Cunha, 8.7) at Gramercy Park), and the (former Mercantile Exchange (1884, Thomas R. Jackson, 3.12) in TriBeCa.)

III. Materiality and the Chromatic Program

The material substance of the elevation constitutes nothing less than a bravura exercise in Victorian polychromatic and polytextural composition. Its dominant field presents a (Warm red brick, crisp bluestone trim), the very pigment of that field consigned to the record with almost forensic exactitude as (Façade Color_Brick Red - 196 47 69), while the base subsides into a crystalline argent tonality inscribed as (Base Color_Crystal - 213 213 213). The masonry is coursed in a (Running Bond) disposition, its trim specified as (Wyoming Bluestone). The whole has been variously extolled as a (red brick and whitestone charmer) and apprehended as a (Queen Anle pile). The comprehensive schedule of the façade's substance enumerates (Brick, Terra Cotta, Wyoming Bluestone, Iron), while the base is constituted of (Red Brick, Wyoming Bluestone.) Certain observers reduce it more laconically to (red brick and whitestone); others to a (red brick) fabric raised (to house the prestigious Century Publishing Company.)

The brickwork disdains monotony. Beyond the plain running-bond field, the composition marshals (Rusticated Brick Pilasters), conferring vertical cadence and the intimation of masonry mass upon the wall plane, and these are interwoven with the ceramic and lapidary ornamental programme that constitutes the building's cardinal glory.

IV. The Principal Elevation: A Bay-by-Bay Anatomization

The Entrance Ensemble

The ground-floor portal is orchestrated as a (3 Bays) disposition of no inconsiderable ceremonial pretension. It is at once (Pedimented) and (Arched), the enveloping arch sustaining a (Fanlight) that suffuses the vestibule with tempered daylight. The aperture is framed by (Tuscan Pilasters), crowned not by the conventional capital proper to that order but by a (Console Bracket Capital) — a quintessentially Queen Anne exercise of grammatical license. The entire surround is enriched with (Cast Iron Ornament), and it is duly recorded that (crisply carved stone surround the ground floor entrance.) The several references are, as observed, conjoined within a thoroughly nonclassical syntax exemplified by the (Jacobean doorway.)

The Fenestration and the Oriel

The presiding conceit of the mid-elevation is a projecting (2-story Oriel), and it is precisely this (two-story oriel window give this Queen Anne style commercial building a welcoming domestic aspect.) Its presence is inscribed, moreover, among the diagnostic hallmarks of the manner, for (The tall oriel window, gambrel roof, prominent chimneys, and molded terra cotta ornament are all characteristic of the style.)

The individual apertures are governed by a (Segmental Arch Lintel (3-5)), each punctuated at its apex by a (Stone Keystone.) The glazing schema is consigned to record as (Window Panes: [1/1] (w/ Transom)) — a solitary light within each of the upper and lower sashes, augmented above by transoms.

The Ornamental Vocabulary

The decorative programme executed in terra cotta and carven stone is nothing short of encyclopaedic, for (Richly detailed terra-cotta panels and crisply carved stone surround the ground floor entrance, embellish the facade, and reappear in the treatment of the top floor dormers.) The lexicon comprehends (Garlands, Shells, Grotesques), together with (Swags), and these figural conceits are marshalled and disciplined by running enrichments — namely a (Dentil Course) and an (Egg & Dart Course.) The building remains throughout (characteristic of the finest Queen Anne design in its free use of classical motifs in stone and terracotta and its employment of the style's trademark sunflowers.)

Presiding over the entire scheme reigns the sunflower — that consecrated emblem of the Aesthetic temperament — rendered here as (Sunflower Terra Cotta Finials) crowning the roofline, their radiant capitula proclaiming the edifice's allegiance to whosoever lifts an eye toward the parapet.

The Roofline and Crowning Members

The building culminates in a (Gambrel Roof), whose domestic and antiquarian resonance amplifies the whole conception. Piercing this covering rise (2-Story Dormers) of arresting prominence, in concert with the (prominent chimneys) whose verticality furnishes the picturesque silhouette indispensable to the manner. The molded ornament thereupon stands corroborated, for the richly detailed panels (reappear in the treatment of the top floor dormers.)

The junction of wall and covering is negotiated by a (Cast Iron Cornice), articulated with its own (Dentils), a (Frieze), and (Skew Corbels) — these last the raking brackets that sustain projecting members along a canted line. A (Balustrade) furnishes a further horizontal cadence, and the whole is punctuated by those (Sunflower Terra Cotta Finials) that constitute its exuberant coronation.

V. The Interior Fabric

Though the building is chiefly celebrated for its exterior, its interior armature was disclosed anew in the course of the modern rehabilitation, which turned to advantage the (high ceilings and such architectural features as Corinthian capped cast-iron columns and consider able original woodwork), an assemblage whose cumulative effect (was impressive.) The reconstituted emporium indeed (featured a college library-like study hall with rows of tables overlooking Broadway attracting students and others.) In its antecedent commercial incarnation the plan was of no lesser consequence, for (The labyrinth of banistered corrals provided well-defined spaces for executives and employees, and it symbolized a well ordered bureaucracy. The pilastered screen with leaded colored-glass windows further departmentalized the workers. Electricity has been brought down to the desk lamps.) That interior served, in its day, as the (OFFICE OF EARL AND WILSON COLLAR MANUFACTURERS), (A business that rode the crest of fashion), whose headquarters of male habiliment (enjoyed a lucrative reign until the whim of fashion changed.)

VI. Chronology of Function and Physical Persistence

The building was conceived as a speculative enterprise, (developed as a speculative venture by the dry-goods company Arnold Constable), and, more particularly, (The Century Building was commissioned as a speculative undertaking by the Arnold and Constable families. In addition to their department store, they were among the largest landholders in late nineteenth-century New York.) Its eponymous and principal tenant conferred upon it its abiding name, for (The building takes its name from the Century Company, publishers of The Century Magazine and St. Nicholas Magazine, which had offices there.) These were, respectively, (the popular Century magazine (for grown-ups) and St. Nicholas (for boys and girls)), issued (before the century turned.) The imprint bore no negligible cultural authority, inasmuch as (The Century was a noted literary publication, carrying work by Mark Twain, Henry James, Walt Whitman, and Herman Melville), its list embracing besides (books by Edith Wharton, Mark Twain, and Rudyard Kipling.) In the fullness of corporate metamorphosis it (joined with D. Appleton & Company in 1933 and. after another merger, became Appleton-Century-Crofts.) Nor did the publisher exhaust the roster of its distinguished occupants, for (Another prominent tenant of the building was the architect George B. Post.)

The successive cadastral surveys attest to the building's physical constancy across the decades, rendering it as (1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building) and (1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building with Store and Elevators), and, in the amplest of these depositions, (1 Structure; 6 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete building with Store and Elevators.) Antecedently the parcel had sustained (4 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Brick or Stone Extension), and, earlier yet, in the cartographic testimony of 1867, the plan bore the appellation (Everett House) upon (5 Structures; Building, Building, Building, Building, Building.) By the early twentieth century the atlases inscribed upon the parcel the solitary word (Lofts.) When the dormer register is reckoned into the account, the building presents itself as a (seven-story) mass.

VII. The Modern Campaign: The 1994 to 1995 Rehabilitation

Consequent upon a protracted interregnum of vacancy, the transformation arrived, for (After standing vacant for many years, the braiding was converted into a bookstore, and the facade received an award-winning restoration.) This supervened upon its formal enrolment among the protected, the building having been (DESIGNATED: OCTOBER 7, 1986), an act elsewhere condensed to the phrase (Designated 1986.) Within the campaign itself, (In 1995 Barnes & Noble transformed the 100,000-square-foot Queen Anne-style Century Building.) The labor was apportioned among several hands: (Judith Saltzman of Li-Saltzman restored the facade, and fames Cohen was charged with maintaining die character of the landmark interiors, which were listed on the National Register, while SBLM Architects. By 3 Group designed the bookstore itself in keeping with the bookseller's overall design standards.) The intervention stands consigned, in the landmark record, to the dates (1880-81; 1994-95), and executed by the (ARCHITECTS: WILLIAM SCHICKEL; LI/ SALTZMAN ARCHITECTS.)

The rehabilitation capitalized, with deliberate discernment, upon the inherited riches of the fabric, engendering an interior whose (effect was impressive, even though it lacked the intimacy and charm of many of the traditional bookstores such as Shakespeare Company and Endicott that the superstores were rapidly driving out of business.)

VIII. The Building in Its Streetscape

The Century Building addresses the plaza from its northern flank and, in the historical dispensation, stood in the fellowship of its neighbors along the square's perimeter, its disposition preserved in a prospect (showing, from left to right the Parish Building (Detlef Lienau, 1883 – 1884), Century Company Building, and Everett house (1854).) It reposes, in the placement of another chronicler, (across the street on the south corner of 17th Street facing Union Square.) Its designation subtended a concerted municipal endeavor, for (Concerns about the fate of historic buildings on other sites around the square led to the designation as landmarks of the Lincoln Building (R. H. Robertson, 1890), 1-3 Union Square West; the Bank of the Metropolis (Bruce Price, 1903), 31 Union Square West; the Century Building (William Schickel, 1881), 33 East Seventeenth Street; the Everett Building (Starrett & Van Vleck, 1908), 45 East Seventeenth Street; and the Germania Life Insurance Company Building (D'Oench &. Yost, 1911), later the Guardian Life Building, 50 Union Square East.)

Critical estimation of its contextual concord has never attained unanimity. Where the preponderance of judgment exalts it as (one of the few extant major Queen Anne buildings in Manhattan), a dissenting strain has maintained that (the Queen Anne pile might have worked somewhere else, it seems oddly uncomfortable here. Union Square can accommodate flamboyant show-offs and it can accommodate derelicts, but not genteel matrons.)

IX. Concluding Assessment

In the final reckoning, the structure at 33 East 17th Street abides as a supremely instructive specimen of the migration of a domestic revivalist idiom into the commercial sphere. Its (Warm red brick, crisp bluestone trim, and a two-story oriel window give this Queen Anne style commercial building a welcoming domestic aspect), while its (Richly detailed terra-cotta panels and crisply carved stone) conspire to fashion a work that endures, in the summary verdict of the record, as (one of the few surviving commercial examples of the Queen Anne style in the city.) The scrupulous rehabilitation of the century's close, wherein (Li/Saltzman Architects) wedded a fastidious façade restoration to a considered interior adaptation, has ensured that this (red brick and whitestone charmer), this (Queen Anle pile), persists at once as a functioning institution and as an irreplaceable document of a pivotal moment in the architectural annals of the city.
