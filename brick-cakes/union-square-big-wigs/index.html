I. From Common Ground to Coveted Address

Every great civic space begins as something humbler, and this one was no exception. In its earliest incarnation the land offered nothing of distinction — (Prior to this, it held squatters and a potter's field.) Its eventual name has long invited misunderstanding, though its true derivation is prosaic rather than patriotic: (Named for the union of Broadway and Fourth Avenue (not the large number of union headquarters historically in the area), Union Square was completed in 1835 as one of several squares set up in Manhattan.) A second chronicler sharpens the point and dispels the persistent myths altogether: (The name "Union" derives not from Civil War days, nor from any association with the labor unions whose activities were later identified with the square, but merely from the fact that this was the "place of union" of the Bowery Road with the northbound Bloomingdale Road.)

What followed was a swift and dramatic ascent in fortune. As speculation took hold, (In the 1840s, the rich bought up the area and lots around the square began selling for ten times their original $500 value.) One by one, the titans of trade raised their houses along the green. Foremost among them was a merchant of glass — (Cornelius van Schaack Roosevelt, an importer of plate glass, lived in a large house at the corner of Broadway and 14th Street, facing the southern edge of the square.) He was soon joined by neighbors of comparable means, for nearby (James F. Penniman, a manufacturer of linseed oil, built a fabulously appointed brownstone mansion at 42 East 14th Street, just west of Roosevelt.) More opulent still was the home of a silk importer, where (Henry Parish, an importer of silk, built an opulent mansion, stuffed with Old Master paintings and featuring an oval stair ascending to a central dome, with an attached conservatory in the side yard, at the southwest corner of Broadway and 17th Street.) The most eccentric residence of all belonged to a pair of brothers, for (Robert Goelet, heir to a family fortune made in hardware, lived directly across 17th Street from Parish, while, two blocks to the north, Goelet's brother Peter built a mysterious brick house set back from the street on one-third of an acre of lawn stocked with strutting peacocks.)

II. The Hour of Union: April 1861

When the guns of Sumter shattered the peace, the square answered with a roar that would echo through the century. In the war's opening days, (On April 20, 1861, a mass rally held here supported Lincoln's call for 75,000 volunteers.) The magnitude of the assembly strained belief — (A crowd estimated at 100,000 to 250,000 people turned out to listen to the 20 dignitaries scheduled to speak that day.) Presiding over this ocean of humanity stood the establishment of the city itself, for (The meeting was presided over by John A. Dix with eighty-seven vice-presidents from the leading men of the community), a roster so illustrious that (The list began with Peter Cooper and ended with John J. Astor.)

Among the voices that rose that afternoon, one carried a tragic prophecy in its wake, for (The most famous of the orators who addressed the meeting was Senator Baker of Oregon, who, during the Mexican War, had led a New York regiment to the gates of the city of Mexico, and who, a few months later, was to give his life for the Union upon the disastrous field of Ball's Bluff, on the soil of the Old Dominion.)

Yet the true emotional summit of the day belonged not to a speaker but to a soldier. All eyes turned to a single weary figure, for (The highlight was the appearance of the Union officer who had been in command of Fort Sumter, Maj. Robert Anderson (1805-1871).) His arrival was still fresh in memory — (Following his surrender on April 13, 1861, he and his men had been allowed to return north aboard the Baltic, arriving in New York harbor on April 19.) His fidelity was all the more moving for the circumstances of his birth, since (Although a Southerner by birth and pro-slavery himself, Anderson remained loyal to the Union. He was promoted to brigadier general and became the North's first national hero.) In symbolic tribute, (The flag that had flown over Fort Sumter was draped over the equestrian statue of George Washington at the south end of the square.) No one present would forget the spectacle, for (The crowd that wildly cheered Anderson that day was the largest ever assembled in the country, and the New York Tribune reponed it as "the greatest popular demonstration ever known in America.")

III. Charity and Valor Amid the Conflict

As the war ground on, the square became a theater of both benevolence and quiet heroism. Its women mounted an extraordinary campaign of relief, for (Also in 1864, the women of the Sanitary Commission hosted a giant charity bazaar called the Metropolitan Sanitary Fair in the square, and in only three weeks raised somewhere between $500,000- $1,000,000 for medical supplies for the troops.) The undertaking sprawled across the district, since (The buildings, erected especially for the occasion, stood on Union Square and along West 14th Street.) At its command stood men whose names still resonate, for (Frederick Law Olmsted, designer of Central Park, served as its general secretary; Henry W. Bellows, pastor of All Souls Unitarian Church, was its president.) One devoted participant would leave posterity an incomparable record, since (One tireless worker for the Commission throughout the war was New York lawyer George Templeton Strong, whose lengthy and readable diary, discovered and published only in the middle of the twentieth century, is one of the major sources for details of New York life in the middle of the nineteenth century, especially during the war years.)

That same year witnessed a scene of profound historical weight. In an act of defiance against prevailing prejudice, (It was also in Union Square on March 5, 1864, that the Union League Club presented the colors to the North's first black regiment, the 20th U.S. Colored Troops.) The moment carried a bitter irony, for (Oddly enough, during the early years of the war the South used the senders of black men but the North did not.) The correction came from private conviction rather than official policy, as (Eventually a group of white citizens, many members of the Union League Club, formed the New York Association for Colored Volunteers and outfitted them.) Their banner proclaimed an unbending creed, for (Their standard was emblazoned with the slogan "Rather Die a Free Man Than Live To Be a Slave.")

The regiment's departure drew a multitude to the streets, for (On March 5, 1864, the 20th st. 1,020 black soldiers marched from the clubhouse to a waiting steamship while tens of thousands lined their route, some cheering and some just gawking.) Their enlistment demanded genuine sacrifice, since (Many of the soldiers had left jobs paying up to sixty dollars a month to take ten dollars (three less than white soldiers) from the army.) Their spiritual guide was a man of remarkable resolve, for (Reverend Henry Highland Garnet served as chaplain while the regiment trained on Rikers Island, but he could not go to the front with them because he limped on a wooden leg, the result of a boyhood injury.)

IV. A City in Mourning

When the nation's leader fell to an assassin, the square that had cheered his war now grieved his passing. In solemn communion, (On April 25, 1865, following Lincoln's death, a massive ecumenical outdoor service was held in the square for the assassinated president.) Clergy of every tradition stood shoulder to shoulder, as (Archbishop John McCloskey, Rabbi Samuel Isaacs, and Reverend Stephen Tyng led the worshippers.) With that gathering an era drew to its close, for (This was the last grand rally of the Civil War era.)

A future president witnessed the sorrow from a nursery window, for it was recorded that (It was from a window on the second floor of that house that six-year-old Theodore Roosevelt, Cornelius's grandson, watched Abraham Lincoln's funeral procession pass slowly down Broadway in 1865.)

V. Grief Cast in Bronze

Out of mourning arose enduring memorials. The city's grief soon sought permanent form, for (Soon after the death of Lincoln, the Union League Club commissioned sculptor Henry Kirke Brown (1814-1886) to create a monument in honor of the slain president.) The work claimed a distinction all its own, since (When this memorial was dedicated in Union Square on September 16,1870, it became the first outdoor statue of Lincoln in America.) Its original ornament has long since vanished, for (Originally the statue was surrounded by a fence that bore an inscription of Lincoln's famous words, "With malice toward none; with charity toward all," but the fence disappeared some unknown time later.) Nor did the artist's design escape reproach, as (Controversy erupted when the statue was unveiled, due to the fact that Lincoln wore a toga over his suit.)

Its companion in bronze had presided over the square for more than a decade already, for (Henry Kirke down's masterful equestrian statue of Washington, one of the earliest pieces of civic sculpture in the city, was unveiled in 1856.) The monument even served an unlikely purpose in its early years, since (A "slave market" for unemployed actors met near the statue in summer, waiting for possible employers.) Its place in the square was not permanent, however, for (At the end of the century the statue was moved to its present location in the center of the square's park.)

A third figure arrived as a gesture of transatlantic gratitude, for (The statue was designed by Frederic-Auguste Bartholdi (who also designed the Statue of Liberty) and is a token of appreciation from the French government for aid provided during the Franco-Prussian War.) That very commission opened the door to his greatest triumph, since (This statue helped Bartholdi win the commission for the Statue of Liberty a few years later.)

A century on, a fourth monument joined the assembly of heroes, for (A statue of Mohandas Gandhi (1869-1948) by Kantilal Patel (1986) stands in the pedestrian triangle at the southwest corner of Union Square.) Its placement was no accident, since (The Parks Department chose this site because of Union Square's tradition of public protests.)

VI. The Nation's Hundredth Birthday

The centennial of the Republic found its stage upon this ground. Years earlier the space had been entrusted to the age's finest hands, for (In 1872 the city hired Calvert Vaux and Frederick Law Olmsted, designers of Central Park, to remake Union Square according to a more up-to-date, picturesque aesthetic.) When the great night arrived, the square could scarcely contain the throng, since (an estimated crowd of 40,000 to 50,000 squeezed into Union Square to await the midnight commencement of the Fourth of July and the nation's Centennial.) The music was as exuberant as it was disorderly, for (The numerous brass bands that had marched in the parade were somehow combined into one group, forming an ungainly orchestra of 600 instruments that managed to play together, more or less, "The Star-Spangled Banner," "Hail the Atlantis," and Beethoven's "The Heavens Are Telling the Glory of God," accompanied by a chorus of 600 singers.) Exuberance soon spilled into peril, for (At the stroke of midnight, fireworks rocketed skyward from each corner of the park. Hats were thrown in the air and pistols fired (at least nine people were accidentally shot).) A venerable poet lent his verse to the occasion, since (Poet William Cullen Bryant, eighty-one, contributed a special "Centennial Ode.")

VII. The Forge of American Dissent

As the century turned, the square assumed a new and combustible identity as the crucible of the nation's labor movement. It hosted a milestone that would reshape the calendar, for (In 1882, the first official labor day parade in the country took place here with nearly twenty-five thousand workers marching in demand of the eight-hour day, an end to both child labor and the contract system.) Its architects harbored ambitions far beyond the eight-hour day, since (The organizers, the Central Labor Union, urged workers to sever all ties to the Democratic and Republican Parties, and unite worldwide.)

The garment trades left their imprint here as well, for (In 1910, the ILGWU organized a rally of five thousand here, during the "Uprising of the Twenty Thousand.") Tragedy soon lent the movement its martyrs, since (The following year, a funeral procession for the victims of the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire marched from Washington Square to Union Square.)

At times the fervor turned to bloodshed. In one calamitous episode, (On March 28, 1908, at a rally of the Socialist Conference of the Unemployed, twenty-two-year-old anarchist Selig Silverstein attempted to throw a homemade bomb at the police after they had clubbed demonstrators.) The design recoiled fatally upon its maker, for (The bomb exploded in Silverstein's hands, killing him and a passerby but leaving the police unharmed.)

The massacre at Ludlow drove the radicals to open provocation. In a gesture of macabre defiance, (Berkman, who had been released from prison eight years earlier, staged a "funeral" for the would-be bombers in Union Square.) The platform drew the leading lights of American radicalism, for (Speakers, including anarchists Rebecca Edelsohn and Charles Plunkett and Wobblies Carlo Tresca and Elizabeth Gurley Flynn, held forth under the watchful eyes of Chief Inspector Max F. Schmittberger and a force of 1,000 police armed with clubs.) One orator issued her creed without apology, for ("I want to say it's about time the working class came out frankly and openly and said 'Yes, we believe in violence,'" Edelsohn, wearing a black dress and red stockings, told the crowd.) Another pressed the argument to its extremity, declaring that ("[i] am for violence," Charles Plunkett shouted. "Not only defensive violence, but offensive violence. I don't believe in waiting until we are attacked.") The seasoned policeman met such fury with dry composure, since ("That was pretty hot stuff," Schmittberger, a Broadway Squad veteran, remarked when Berkman finished.)

The same firebrand returned to name the guilty a second time, for (Carlo Tresca spoke on International Worker's Day in 1914, denouncing those who killed thirty-three workers in the Ludlow, Colorado strike at the Rockefeller-owned Colorado Fuel and Iron Company.) His indictment reached its theatrical climax when ("Name the Murderer!" he thundered and the crowd called back "Rockefeller!")

An earlier agitator had already paid dearly for her eloquence, for (In 1893, Emma Goldman was arrested for a speech to the unemployed, wherein she urged those without bread to take it.) Her confinement proved unexpectedly formative, since (She spent a year in jail at Blackwell's Island (now Roosevelt Island), learning the nursing trade; she later earned two medical degrees in Europe.)

VIII. The High-Water Mark of the Red Tide

The Depression summoned to the square a gathering of unprecedented scale. It was born of a nationwide call, for (One of the most famous demonstrations in Union Square was in 1930, when, during the depression, the Communist Party called for nation-wide demonstrations against unemployment.) The city's police commissioner recalled its genesis with precision, noting that (Early in March of 1930 we received a report from our Police Intelligence Squad that 100,000 postal cards had been sent out to members of the Communist Party summoning them to a meeting to be held March 6 at noon in Union Square.) His summons to the movement's leaders met with flat rejection, for (I was told by my three guests that they did not respect the laws of the United States and the State of New York, or the ordinances of the City of New York, and that they would not make any application for a permit to hold a meeting in Union Square.) The multitude that assembled dwarfed all precedent, since (Over 100,000 people had congregated around Union Square by noon. It was one of the largest crowds ever seen there and was the high-water mark in Red demonstrations.)

A symbolic affront flared briefly before the authorities intervened, for (When the committee went back to the crowd it was as if by magic that six portable stands were put up for the speakers and the Soviet flag run up over the American flag.) The response came at once, since (The police immediately corrected this error and ordered that the flags be reversed before any speakers could address the audience.) The rhetoric of the day fell chiefly upon the commissioner himself, for (Most of the oratory was addressed against "Whalen's Cossacks" and their brutality.) When the crowd sought to march, it found every avenue sealed, since (The police cut off movement to the south entirely, so in the end the demonstrators never got below 14th Street.)

A generation later, one final protest brushed the square's edges, for (On lune 19, 1953, since the police refused to let organizers use Union Square, several thousand crowded Seventeenth Street adjoining the square to protest the impending execution of the Rosenbergs.)

IX. The Silver Prophet of Union Square

In the century's latter half, the square drew a legend of an altogether different order. Abandoning uptown for the district's bohemian energy, (Artist Andy Warhol moved to the fifth floor of this twelve-story building in 1968.) His new sanctum bore a name that would become synonymous with the age, since (The white-walled loft space was called The Factory and housed studios, his office, and a screening room for his films.) The building's history proved curiously fitting, for (The eleven-story Decker Building on Union Square was designed by John Edelmann, an architect known to have anarchist sympathies, for the Decker Piano Co.) Its guardianship was famously whimsical, since (A stuffed Great Dane (which Warhol hinted had belonged to Cecil B. DeMille—though the staff could never match it to any photos of DeMille's dogs) stood next to the entrance as their lone security measure.)

Then came the day that nearly ended everything, for (On June 3, 1968, Valerie Solanas, an occasional Factory visitor and sole member of SCUM (Society for Cutting Up Men), came to Warhol's office here, pulled a revolver and shot point blank at him, hitting the artist in the chest.) Against every expectation he clung to life, since (Warhol was pronounced dead at the hospital but, miraculously, managed to survive.) The reckoning that followed was swift yet lenient, for (The crazed Solanas surrendered to the police a few hours after the shooting. She served a three-year sentence and died in 1988. Warhol lived until 1987.)

Outgrowing his quarters, the artist relocated only steps away, for (By 1974, Warhol had run out of space at his Union Square studio called The Factory and, in August of that year, he moved across the street to this six-story brick building.) Ever the pragmatist, he transformed the move into a ritual, since (Always economical, Warhol made it a condition that all visitors to The Factory during the move had to carry at least one item across the street to the new building.) From the disorder of relocation sprang an enduring artwork, for (One of the first was accidental: Warhol's Time Capsules.) In these rooms he embraced the coming generation, since (He reenergized his image by producing artwork with younger artists of the time, notably Keith Haring, Jean-Michel Basquiat, and Francesco Clemente.)

X. The Solitary Master

Decades before Warhol's arrival, another painter labored in obscurity along the square's eastern flank. In a quiet devotion to his craft, (Painter Arshile Gorky worked on his art in a studio on this site from 1929 until his death in 1948.) His journey to New York had begun far from these streets, for (Gorky was an Armenian born in Turkey who came to New York in 1925.) He regarded his workplace with near-religious reverence, since (Gorky treated his working space here as a sacred area—he scrubbed the floor weekly, stocked it with the best materials, and hung life-size photographs of his favorite masters on the wall.) His story ended in sorrow, for (Gorky committed suicide by hanging in Connecticut on July 21, 1948 at the age of forty-eight.)

XI. Merchants, Money, and a Gangster's Last Stand

The commercial spirit reshaped the square with equal drama. In a single stroke of demolition and construction, (IN 1870, TIFFANY'S DEMOLISHED the James Renwick-designed Church of the Puritans on the west side of Union Square and replaced it with a huge cast-iron store-the "Palace of Jewels.") The booksellers followed, chief among them a man of humble beginnings, for (It is interesting to note that Agosto Brentano, who came to America as a poor cripple from Sicily, eked out a living selling newspapers and periodicals, and ultimately became the most famous book dealer in the country.)

Finance planted its roots here as well, for (The bank was organized in 1871, and commenced operations in June of that year.) Its governing board read like a directory of the age's commercial aristocracy, since its members numbered (Charles L. Tiffany, of Tiffany & Company; Hon. Samuel Sloan, President of the Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad; Robert Schell, the President; Joseph Park, of Park & Tilford; William Steinway, of Steinway & Sons; William B. Isham, capitalist; W. D. Sloane, of W. & J. Sloane; and Hicks Arnold, of Arnold, Constable & Company.) Close at hand, a hotel became the deathbed of a celebrated thinker, for (The single-tax economist Henry George died here on October 29, 1897.)

And on Fourteenth Street a notorious criminal met his violent end. Having sought redemption after the Great War, (By 1920, former gang leader Monk Eastman was trying to lead a life along the straight and narrow.) Yet his reform could not spare him, since (So, he got involved with a crooked Prohibition agent, who on December 26 of that year shot and killed Eastman in a drunken dispute on the sidewalk outside this address.)

XII. Literary Shades of the Side Streets

The quieter blocks flanking the square harbored their own distinguished ghosts. Among them, a scion of old New York breathed his last, for (That sturdy descendant of the Knickerbockers, Gulian C. Verplanck, died at 12 East Sixteenth Street.) Another author lodged nearby and, in a letter home, captured the wry contentment of old age, confiding that ("I see a great deal of Mark Twain nowadays," he wrote his sister from this address, "and we have good times denouncing even thing. We are old fellows, and it is pleasant to find the world so much worse than it was when we were young.")
