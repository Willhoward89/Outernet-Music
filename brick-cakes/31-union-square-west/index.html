I. The Land Before the Building

In the beginning the plot was empty. (In the 1815 Blue Book, on 31 Union Square West, there is reference to 0 structures.)

The larger district around it had already caught the eye of the shrewd. (Long before an axe cut into the first elderly tree on the future Washington Square, well-informed real estate speculators—Astors, Goelets and Schermerhorns in the lead—had bought property to the north. The block between Sixteenth and Seventeenth streets, from Union Square to Fifth Avenue sold for $197,000 in 1836, the lots that fronted on Fifth Avenue were valued at $57,000 each. Union Place had been, early in the nineteenth century, an exclusive suburb for the country villas of Goelets and Roosevelts whose town houses stood near the tip of the island. Their park was surrounded by a massive iron fence which lasted until 1870, when the Place became the Square, already part of the flourishing theater center that had forced out the country houses.)

The character of the surrounding streets was shifting fast. (The small eighteenth-century shops of Cherry Street and Catherine Street that had swelled and moved to larger and more fashionable quarters on Broadway were now beginning to ring this upper square. Tiffany moved its five hundred craftsmen and sales people into a new iron building at Fifteenth and Broadway — "hideous," some thought the style, which was tolerated by others because it found use for the excess iron and ironworkers left unemployed at the close of the Civil War—and Brentano's very literary emporium came to rest for a while at 33 Union Square.)

By mid-century the lot had filled with fine masonry. (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 31 Union Square West, there is reference to 3 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Brick or Stone Extension, Brick or Stone Store first class, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only.) A little more than a decade later the tally shrank, for (In the 1867 Dripps Plan, on 31 Union Square West, there is reference to 2 structures; Building, Building.)

II. Early Occupants

The address quietly hosted businesses. An architectural partnership resided there: (BOGART & CUYLER / John Bogart, John Y. Cuyler / 1874, 31 Union Sq.) Later the site bore a manufacturer's name, as (In the 1885 Robinsons Atlas, on 31 Union Square West, there is written 'White Manfg.Co.' as well as reference to 2 Structures; Brick building, Brick building.) By the end of the century a modest structure stood, since (In the 1897 Bromleys Atlas, on 31 Union Square West, there is reference to 1 Structure; 4 Story Brick building.)

The moment itself was captured by a camera nearby: (The photograph above, of a cabby and his hansom, was taken in Union Square, at Broadway and Sixteenth Street, in 1896.)

III. The Bank and Its Founders

The institution destined to define the address had deep local roots. (The Bank of the Metropolis is a flourishing outgrowth of the movement of business to the up-town section of New York. Union Square, where its banking house is established, was thirty years ago a fashionable residence-district. To-day it is surrounded by some of the largest retail business houses in New York, and important manufacturing and wholesale industries are plentiful in the neighborhood.)

It began with modest men and modest quarters: (The bank was organized in 1871, and commenced operations in June of that year. The first President was W. A. Kissam (who died in the same year), and the original place of business was 31 Union Square. A removal to 17 Union Square followed six years later, and in 1888 the bank took the more commodious quarters at 29 Union Square, which it now occupies.)

Its leadership was distinguished: (Robert Schell, the President, who has held the position steadily for twenty years, was formerly a well-known jewelry merchant in Maiden Lane. William B. Isham, the Vice-President (since 1885), was prominent in the leather trade ; and the Cashier, Theodore Rogers, has occupied the same position since the formation of the bank.) Its board glittered with names of consequence: (They are Charles L. Tiffany, of Tiffany & Company; Hon. Samuel Sloan, President of the Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad; Robert Schell, the President; Joseph Park, of Park & Tilford; William Steinway, of Steinway & Sons; William B. Isham, capitalist; W. D. Sloane, of W. & J. Sloane; and Hicks Arnold, of Arnold, Constable & Company.)

Its finances were robust, for (The bank has a deposit line of nearly $7,000,000; and a surplus of $700,000 has been accumulated on the capital of $300,000. The shares of the Bank of the Metropolis have a market value of over $400 each.) In the directories of the era it appeared simply as (Bank of the Metropolis, 31 Union Square.) The building's very foundation was rooted in commerce: (Founded in 1871, the Bank of the Metropolis was formed to serve the needs of the businesses located around Union Square. In order to attract the business of leading publishers, jewelers, and other merchants, the board of directors included such powerful neighborhood businessmen as Louis Comfort Tiffany and Charles Scribner.)

IV. The Skyscraper Rises

Then came the architect and the tower. (No. 31, the former Bank of the Metropolis (Bruce Price, 1902), is a fine example of the architect's favored Renaissance Revival style. Although the building's front features polished marble columns, the entire structure is, in effect, columnar or tripartite in form, with a base, shaft, and capital represented by its three stone sections.) It was (An early and unwitting sliver building, this neo-Renaissance slab now houses a restaurant in its original banking rooms, a place for SUPERMAN'S lunch).

The skyscraper announced a changing district: (The sixteen story skyscraper, designed by Bruce Price In 1901, was symbolic of the tall structures invading ladies Mile In the early twentieth century and hastening the retreat of the fashionable shopping district up Fifth Avenue above Thirty-fourth Street.) Beside it stood a neighbor, (The Union Building, a Moorish Venetian structure designed by Alfred Zucker In 1891.)

The neighborhood it entered was already crowded with the garment trade: (More than a dozen ladies' tailoring establishments could he found in the 1900 business directories of New York, in close proximity to the new Waldorf Astoria Hotel.) The tower's upper reaches teemed with trades, for (The following year. Bruce Price designed the sixteen-story office tower for the Bank of the Metropolis on the northwest corner of Sixteenth Street and Union Square West. Here, loo. The upper floors were rented to at least three shirtwaist-makers, three different ladies and children's undergarment manufacturers, in addition to six different architectural offices.) And the surrounding streets followed suit: (The side streets near ladies Mile were also invaded by lofty buildings used to house the expanding garment industry that now reached as far north as Thirty-Sixth Street. Ten and twelve-story loft buildings, sometimes as narrow as one city lot, were erected by the hundreds, flooding the fashionable shopping district with thousands of sweatshop workers who had little extra money to purchase the goods in the fancy shop windows along Ladies Mile, but lent the area an unsavory quality.)

The tower itself embodied a great change in the cityscape, for (What doomed the format, besides changing tastes, was the arrival of skyscrapers, which put an end once and for all to a city of roughly uniform building heights.)

V. The Critics Weigh In

Critics admired its composition. (This narrow old bank is one of the city's more appealing smaller towers, a lovely eclectic creation. There is a wonderful, gently bowed Ionic portico at the bottom, a slender, almost Sleek shaft, and an enormous, elaborate cornice. It feels like a building Louis Sullivan would have designed had he been a Beaux-Arts architect; some of the ornament even seems a watered down version of Sullivan's, although the overall aesthetic is clearly a more classical and academic one. Price never quite found a style and never did a truly great building, but he was a deft, intuitive maker of compositions, and this is one of his best works.) That same account paired the address with its neighbor, noting (The Bank of metropolis now 31 Union Square West and 33 Union Square West.)

Another judged that (Its Union Square front is extremely narrow Between an Ionic portico and a heavy cornice is a sleek, tastefully ornamented shaft; as with other Price buildings, it is a forerunner of the stripped classicism later practiced by such architects as Paul Philippe Cret.) In photographic surveys it was placed within its block as (MIDDLE EAT SIDE 3. Union Square West, Nos 31-41.)

VI. The Architecture in Detail

The building spoke a rich classical language. (Occupying a commanding corner location, the building superbly demonstrates Bruce Prices ability to use the requirements of function, the dictates of site, and a classical vocabulary to create a skyscraper that emanates authority. The neo-Renaissance limestone rower is enhanced by classical elements traditionally associated with American bank architecture—most notably, a bowed two-story portico with monumental polished-granite columns, lions' heads, consoles, and foliated spandrels.)

The design logic followed the column itself. (The site demanded a long, thin building, and Price's vision related the skyscraper to a classical column. The tripartite scheme—a rusticated base, a nine-story midsection, and a capital topped by a prominent copper cornice—is a commanding visual, rather than functional, solution to organizing a tall structure.) The architect had a philosophy: (Price, an influential architect of skyscrapers, once remarked that "their aerial aspect [is] of more value to the city as a whole than the distorted partial values that, as a rule, are all we can obtain from the street.") And so (His neo-Classical vocabulary fuses well here with the dictates of skyscraper construction to create a distinctly modern, urban building.)

Landmark scholarship echoed this reading: (Price was an important early skyscraper designer with a particular interest in adapting the tripartite form of the column Io tad buildings. This neo-Renaissance limestone faced example is clearly massed in Price's preferred base-shaft-capital manner.)

The most granular catalogue of the façade reads: (neo-Renaissance, Base Color Coral Reef - 195 185 154, Façade Color Pearl White - 238 235 218, Base Material Rusticated Limestone, Granite Bulkheads, Polished Granite Columns, Façade Material Limestone, Entrance Description 3-Bays, 2-Story Bowed Portico, Granite Ionic Columns, Lions' Heads, Console Brackets, Foliated Spandrels, Broken Pediment, Lion-head Waterspouts, Frieze, Window Detail Square-headed, Stone Surround (3), Foliated Spandrels: Acanthus/ Roundel Centers (Rest), Open-mouthed Lions (10), Brickwork Style Rusticated Pilasters, Round Headed Arches, Smooth Faced Pilasters, Swags, Egg & Dart Echinus, Tripartite Typology, Roofline Description Scrolled Consoles, Wave/ 'Running Dog', Projecting Bandcourse, Palmettes, Copper Cornice, Dentils, Modillions.)

VII. The Building on the Maps

The atlases tracked the finished tower. (In the 1916 Atlas of the Borough of Manhattan, on 31 Union Square West, there is written 'Bank of the Metropolis' as well as reference to 2 Structures; 16 Story Brick building with Store and Elevators and Fire proof building (steel construction) and 1 Story Brick Extension.) A later record noted a taller count: (In the 1930 Land Book of the Borough of Manhattan, on 31 Union Square West, there is written 'Bank of the Metropolis' as well as reference to 2 Structures; 17 Story Brick building with Elevators and Fire proof building (steel construction).)

VIII. Mergers and New Names

The bank did not remain independent. (The bank was absorbed by the Bank of the Manhattan Company in 1918; the resulting entity merged with Chase in 1955 to become the Chase Manhattan Bank, now JP Morgan Chase & Co.) Concisely put elsewhere: (The bank, founded in 1871, served local businesses until 1918, when it merged with the Bank of the Manhattan Company (now J. P. Morgan Chase).) And again: (The Bank of the Metropolis, founded m 1871 to serve the needs of businesses in the Union Square area, maintained its offices on the square until 1918. when it was absorbed by the Bank of Manhattan.)

The successor's name appeared in directories: (STATE BANKS. Bk. of Metropolis, 31 Union sq.) and later (STATE BANKS. Manhattan Co., 40 Wall, 31 Union sq., 333 Madison av.) A portrait painter even gave it as her mailing point: (Address: care Bank Manhattan Co., 31 Union Sq., New York 3, N.Y.) The maps confirmed the change of ownership, as (In the 1955 Manhattan Land Book, on 31 Union Square West, there is written 'Chase Manhattan Sank' as well as reference to 1 Structure; 16 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete building with Store and Elevators and Fire proof building (steel construction).)

IX. Redevelopment Pressures and Landmark Status

The surrounding block faced the developer's eye. (In June 1930, the Klein estate was reportedly in negotiations with five developers seeking to purchase the site and its buildings. But it was not until 1983, when William Zeckendorf Jr., son of the legendary developer of the 1950s and 1960s who had died in 1976, acquired a two-year option on the site, that progress began to be made. Zeckendorf retained Davis, Brody & Associates to develop plans for the block's redevelopment as a cluster of four superscaled apartment towers set atop a seven-story base.)

The plans stirred resistance. (To realize the Zeckendorf plan, the site needed to be rezoned, a move that was rigorously opposed by the Union Square Community Coalition, a group largely made up of newer residents who had moved into lofts and apartments and feared the impact of real estate speculation on their rented homes.) In the end, (Both the district and the Zeckendorf proposal for a twenty-six-story project were approved by the City Planning Commission in November 1984 and by the Board of Estimate in January 1985.)

Out of this ferment came protection. (Concerns about the fate of historic buildings on other sites around the square led to the designation as landmarks of the Lincoln Building (R. H. Robertson, 1890), 1-3 Union Square West; the Bank of the Metropolis (Bruce Price, 1903), 31 Union Square West; the Century Building (William Schickel, 1881), 33 East Seventeenth Street; the Everett Building (Starrett & Van Vleck, 1908), 45 East Seventeenth Street; and the Germania Life Insurance Company Building (D'Oench &. Yost, 1911), later the Guardian Life Building, 50 Union Square East.)

The official record states it plainly: ((FORMER) BANK OF THE METROPOLIS 1902-03; 1979 CONVERSION 31 UNION SQUARE WEST (ALSO KNOWN AS 19-23 EAST 16TH STREET), MANHATTAN ARCHITECT: BRUCE PRICE DESIGNATED: JULY 12, 1988.)

X. A New Life

The tower was reborn for modern use. (Today the former banking floor is a restaurant, Blue Water Grill, and many of the upper floors were converted to apartments in 1979.) As one guide summarized, (A restaurant occupies the main floor with residences above.) The restaurant appears in listings simply as (Blue Water Grill (31 Union Square W)) and among seafood destinations as (Blue Water Grill (31 Union Square W): Seafood, Sunday…95). A music venue also lived there, listed under (WHERE TO HEAR METROPOLIS, 31 Union Sq W, 675-0851), and later remembered among the closed establishments simply as (Metropolis) and (Blue Water Grill).

XI. The Neighboring Table — 21 East 16th Street

Across the way, the address of 21 East 16th Street told a parallel food story. Once it was a vegetarian haunt, where (Brownie's became a destination for health fanatics, vegetarians, Factory associates, and Warhol after his shooting in 1968 when his doctor told him to keep an eye on his diet.) In time, (Danny Meyer's Union Square Cafe now occupies the Brownie's address.)

The café itself was a landmark of dining: (Union Square Cafe became one of the final transforming presences in the neighborhood when it opened in 1985, a natural extension of a revamped park and a tremendously successful greenmarket.) Its layout was distinctive: (Danny Meyer's restaurant is broken into four precincts in two halves: half one, as you enter, includes the long dining bar (featuring oyster and wine specials, and the full menu), and two dining areas beyond (an airy room with tall walls, and a railing balcony); half two, on the west, is the large sunken dining room with a cherry floor and dark green wainscoting. Chef Michael Romano gives the primarily American menu lots of Italian and French touches.)

It was found among the closed bars as (Union Square Café) and celebrated for its (Publishing lunch crowd. Noisy, high-ceilinged rooms. Expensive.) Its coordinates were recorded as (Union Square Café 21 E. 16th St. btwn Fifth Ave. & Union Sq. W. 212.243.4020 L; N. R; 4. 5.6 at 14th St. -Union Sq.) and as (Union Square Cafe*, 21 E. 16th St. (Fifth Ave./Union Sq. W.), 243-4020, $E; Contemporary.) Its dishes lingered in guidebooks — (Potatoes, mashed), (Pie, key lime), (Fish, tuna steak), (Soup, black bean), and (Game) — each paired with (Union Square Cafe (21 E 16th St)).
