From Open Ground to a First-Class Row

The story of this parcel begins, appropriately, with nothing at all. The earliest surviving record, drawn from the ledgers of the pre-Civil War city, confirms that here there was (reference to 0 structures)—a bare frontage on a street that had not yet become the fashionable artery it would soon be. Within a generation, however, the ground had filled dramatically. By mid-century the block bristled with elegant construction, the cartographers recording no fewer than twelve substantial dwellings of the highest grade, each classified as a (Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Brick or Stone Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only)—a phrase repeated, with only slight architectural variation, for structure after structure along the row. These were not tenements but the residences of the comfortable, their coped roofs and top-floor skylights signaling both prosperity and taste.

By the post-war years the block had grown denser still, the surveyors now counting fifteen anonymous structures and inscribing upon the plan the single most telling name of the era: (L. Del monico). The presence of the Delmonico family was no accident, for the street had become synonymous with refinement—a fact confirmed decades later by a biographer who situated the neighborhood (just a few doors down from the fashionable Delmonico's Restaurant, on Fourteenth Street at the corner of Fifth Avenue at that time).

The Reign of the Demorests

If any single enterprise defined the block in its golden commercial age, it was the fashion empire of the Demorests, whose names the atlas-makers dutifully recorded across the parcel: (W.C Demorest, W.Jennings Demorest, W.Jennings Demorest). The tale of Ellen Louise Curtis Demorest reads, in the words of one chronicler, like the trajectory of a woman who (had lived almost literally a movie star's life). Born in the northern reaches of the state, she was a figure of remarkable enterprise—a woman recorded in the annals as a (Business woman and philanthropist), who (worked as a milliner and with her husband. William Jennings Demorest, built a fashion empire in New York City that included Madame Demorest's Fashion Emporium).

Her rise was methodical. Having been set up in her first shop upstate, she (had progressed from there to Troy, to Philadelphia, and finally to New York City, the fashion capital of the country), moving her operation, by stages, (up from Grand Street to Twelfth Street, then to 17 East Fourteenth Street). The chronology of her emporium is preserved precisely: from 1860 to 1887 she operated her establishment (successively at 473 Broadway, 838 Broadway and 17 east 14th street). Her husband, ever the speculator, (invested heavily in property along Fourteenth Street andf moved his Demorest Emporium of Fashion to 17 East Fourteenth Street in 1874).

The genius of the enterprise lay in a single, deceptively simple innovation. It was Ellen who (first displayed two dozen different paper patterns for women's dresses in her Philadelphia home in the 1850s, marking the beginning of a fantastic empire), and their method of (grading and labelling the patterns with an illustration of the garment, and full directions for its use) had, in the confident language of their own advertising, (revolutionized the business pattern). The firm styled itself in the grandest terms as the (MME. DEMOREST'S COSMOPOLITAN EMPORIUM OF FASHION, 17 East 14th Street, N. Y.), promising customers (Furnishing Economy, Practical Utility, Artistic Accuracy, and Fashionable Elegance). Through their pattern trade and their publications—among them (Demorest's Illustrated Monthly Magazine and Mme Demorest mirur of fashions)—the latest silhouettes of Paris and Vienna reached parlors across the continent.

The couple's convictions extended well beyond commerce. In a gesture radical for its day, Ellen (hired African American women and treated them as equals, telling customers who objected, no matter how wealthy, to go elsewhere). She was, moreover, (a founding member of Sorosis, the first women's club in the nation). Yet even empires wane: by the time of her retirement in 1883, the record notes with quiet finality, (Demorest's patterns had been largely supplanted by those of E. Butrerick and Company).

An Architect's Office and a Culture of Music

Fashion was not the block's only trade. At the neighboring number, an ambitious young designer kept his premises, advertising himself with the address (OFFICE: No. 15 East 14th Street, New York City) and describing his professional priorities as (FINE RESIDENCIS, STORE AND OFFICE BUILDINGS). This was C. B. J. Snyder—a man whose reputation, before his celebrated municipal career, already extended to (commercial alterations he did while working for the Demorests)—before, in due course, (J. Oscar Bunce took over his private practice).

The block was equally a seat of music. In an age when (at all concerts the grand piano bore the maker's name on the side, in gilt Gothic letters about a foot high), the piano manufacturers clustered here in force. The firm of Chickering and Sons (had warehouses at 11 East Fourteenth street in the 1860s), and the same address housed a modest but cultivated venue—(Steck Hall, at 11 East Fourteenth Street, a small place of chamber-music size), a room numbered among those halls (used mainly for concerts and recitals).

A Whisper of Notoriety

Beneath the block's veneer of gentility lay at least one strand of scandal. Before her marriage and her later infamy in one of the most sensational trials in British criminal history, (Florence Maybrick lived for a time at 17 East 14th Street)—the same transplanted American who, in 1889, (was convicted of murdering her husband by arsenic poisoning), a case that (became an international cause celebre) and drew appeals for clemency from no less than (Presidents Grover Cleveland and William McKinley).

The Birth of an Art Form

It is for a single, transformative episode, however, that this address earned its place in cultural history. In 1906 a Victorian brownstone here—the so-called (Cunard Mansion)—became the second home of a pioneering film concern, and it was here, on (the northern fringe of Greenwich Village), that (the birth of the modern film industry took place). The company installed its production facilities in (a 50-foot-wide brownstone mansion), converting its most elegant chamber, so that (The old ballroom served as a studio for many of the fledgling silent films that were first made in New York City).

The technological daring of the enterprise cannot be overstated. This was (the first motion picture studio in the world to rely exclusively on artificial lighting, utilizing long tubular lights to illuminate the stage)—a distinction the record elsewhere phrases as (the first motion picture studio in the world to rely exclusively on electric lights for illumination of the stage). It was in this converted ballroom that a former stage actor, (a young actor hired in 1907), came into his own. Having (been turned down for work by Edison Studios), D. W. Griffith found his vocation here, and (directed his first movie, The Adventures of Dollie, here in 1908)—a picture that (premiered at the Union Square Theatre on July 14. 1908). One biographer left a vivid portrait of the director at work: (In a wide-brimmed desperado hat, with piercing blue eyes and a bony prow of a nose, he was everywhere - brainstorming a whole movie on the spot, driving his actors, constantly experimenting).

His productivity was prodigious. In his first year alone (Griffith directed forty-nine short subjects), and across his tenure he became the studio's top director, (making over 500 short films). The roster of talent that passed through these doors reads as a founding pantheon of American cinema; among those who (began their careers here) were (Mary Pickford, Lionel Barrymore, Lillian and Dorothy Gish, Mabel Normand, and director Mack Sennett)—a company later expanded in memory to include (John Bunny... Maurice Costello. Flora Finch, Wallace Reid, Norma Talmadge, Clara Kimball Young, Mac Marsh. Francis X. Bushman. Beverly Bayne and Blanche Sweet). Pickford herself, then a young woman of eighteen, made her debut in short features (churned out on the average of one per week, beginning with Her First Biscuits in 1909).

The idyll was finite. When Griffith departed in 1913 (to work for the Mutual Film Corporation,) and ultimately (to found his own production company and eventually make The Birth of a Nation (1915)), the studio's great chapter closed. The company relocated, and in time the building itself was lost—(The brownstone studio here on East 14th street was demolished in the1960s). A belated tribute proved almost farcically ephemeral: in 1975 a plaque was dedicated by two surviving veterans of the studio, (former Biograph beauties Lillian Gish, and Blanche Sweet), yet (The day after the ceremony, however, the plaque mysteriously disappeared, and there have been no further efforts to put up a new one).

The Modern Palimpsest

The twentieth century steadily overwrote these earlier lives. By mid-century a financial institution occupied the ground, the land book recording simply (AMALGAMATED BANK). Then, in a decisive act of erasure and renewal, the present structure rose: it (Was built in 1965), an edifice christened, with a certain irony given its idiom, (VICTORIA, THE). One later guide surveyed it with faint bemusement as a (huge white-brick apartment house of the 1960s—hardly Victorian in feeling), one that (probably honors England's queen, who ruled from 1837 until 1901).

At street level the commerce of daily life carried on in its endlessly renewing forms—a pet supplier, (PETLAND DISCOUNTS); a beauty establishment praised for its (Indonesian flavor); and a gourmet market of the sort described as one of the (real farmer's and gourmet markets) whose stores are (immaculate and well organized). Thus a single frontage, once empty ground, became in turn a row of first-class dwellings, a fashion empire, a temple of music, the improbable cradle of an entire art form, and at last an ordinary apartment house—each stratum overlaid upon the last, the whole a compressed history of the changing city itself.
