I. Three Houses at the Landing: The Colonial Foundations

The story of the building that now occupies 49 Main Street begins in the earliest days of European settlement on the eastern reach of Long Island, at a moment when the future village was scarcely more than a scattering of homesteads clinging to the edge of the bay. The historical record is unambiguous that Sag Harbor starting to grow in 1730, when there were at the Landing but three houses, those of John Foster, (west side Main street), James Howell (site of American Hotel) Daniel Fordham (corner Main and Noyac streets). From the very first, then, this particular parcel was bound up with one of the three foundational dwellings of the nascent port, a distinction that confers upon it a near-unrivaled antiquity within the village's built fabric. Indeed, chroniclers were later confident that this was probably one of the earliest inhabited locations in Sag Harbor.

The man whose name is forever welded to the site was a figure of genuine substance in the colonial community. James Howell was one of the early settlers of Southampton and among the first men to locate in Sag Harbor, about 100 years after the original laying out and occupation of the township. His pedigree and dates are recorded with precision: He was born at Southampton, L. I., October 15, 1734. When a young man he moved to Sag Harbor; there he died December 12, 1808. His holdings were considerable, for He owned and resided on site of the property now occupied by the American Hotel and Jetter block, in Sag Harbor, and his proprietorship extended well beyond this single lot—He also owned the meadows on the west side of Main street. So deeply did Howell's name adhere to the location that the village's antiquarians fixed it there as a point of memory: James Howell kept tavern where the American House now is. He was among the first settlers of Sag Harbor, and in a poignant detail of domestic loss that doubles as a chronological anchor, His child is the first interment at Old Burying Ground, Madison street, opened 1767.

II. The Officer in the Bed: The Shadow of Revolution

If the tavern's first half-century was one of quiet accumulation, the Revolutionary War thrust it abruptly onto the stage of military history. When the British seized Long Island, Howell's establishment became a nerve center of the occupying garrison, for Officers were quartered in the house and tavern of James Howell, which stood on the site of the present American Hotel. The strategic logic of the quartering was deliberate and plain: Here they were close enough to have full view of their vessels anchored in the harbor. There is a certain irony, carefully preserved by the record, in the political allegiance of the proprietor himself, since James Howell, who by the way was a Tory in those days, like most of his neighbors who subscribed to the oath of allegiance to the King.

The great reversal came in the celebrated raid of 1777, when the daring Colonel Return Jonathan Meigs descended upon the port. The decisive stroke unfolded at Howell's very door: Two guards were captured and forced to lead the detatchment to the home of James Howell on Main Street, where the British commanding officer had been staying. There he was arrested as he lay in his bed. The episode entered the historical canon as a signal act of audacity, for it was here that Col. Meigs, in his expedition to Sag Harbor, during the Revolutionary war (1777) took the British commander a prisoner from his quarters, at the Howell dwelling, the site of the American Hotel. Amid the chaos of bombardment the family endured a harrowing episode of its own, when James Howell put his daughter, afterwards Mrs. Abigail Price, in his cellar for safety. Tradition long held that the occupation exacted a physical toll upon the structure, suggesting that Perhaps at this time the house was burned, which tradition imputed to the British soldiers.

III. A Thousand Barrels and a Parade Ground: The Coffee House and the Conflagration

As the new republic matured, the establishment shed its colonial identity and assumed a more genteel character suited to the age. In the early nineteenth century Its name was changed to Eldredge's Coffee House in the early 1800s. It became a fixture of civic and ceremonial life, and local lore embellished it with both a prodigious cellar and a martial clientele, the building being remembered as the gathering place of the old military group, the Suffolk Guards, who would assemble there when parades were held in the village. Yet the edifice that had survived war could not survive fire. The catastrophic blaze that swept the business district proved its undoing, for the building was lost in the great fire of 1845, only to be rebuilt in brick in the Italianate style by Sag Harbor's furniture maker, Nathan Tinker. The loss was reckoned grievous, as It was a great loss to the village.

IV. The Cabinetmaker's Phoenix: Nathan Tinker's Brick Resurrection

From the ashes rose the structure that endures to this day, the work of one of the village's most accomplished artisans. Nathan Tinker was one of Sag Harbor's early craftsmen whose specialty was cabinetmaking and whose furniture was of such fine quality and workmanship that some of it still exists today. His personal particulars are preserved: Tinker, born in 1792, grew up in Sag Harbor and married Hannah P. Woodward. The site had long been his place of labor, for From 1825 until the fire of 1845, Nathan Tinker had his cabinetmaking shop on this site. After the disaster he reconceived the lot as a domestic rather than commercial space, inasmuch as After the fire he decided to build a residence here. The result was no hotel at all in its first incarnation but a dwelling of dual purpose: The American Hotel, completed in 1846, was originally a two-family home, split down the middle, for Tinker and his son. The partnership was a family affair, for Following the fire, Tinker made his son Samuel a partner. They renovated the store and added a large brick structure that today is the American Hotel, cherishing the hope that they might one day convert the building into a double dwelling for themselves. Tinker did not long enjoy his handiwork; Nathan Tinker remained in the furniture making business for twenty-nine years, and in 1847 at the age of 55, he passed away. He is buried in Oakland Cemetery. His architectural legacy is legible in the fine detailing that the building shares with its neighbors—one is urged to Notice the drip lintels above the windows, a feature also found on the other two buildings.

V. Electric Lights and a Hundred-Foot Whale: The Birth of a Hotel

The transformation of Tinker's brick residence into the hostelry recognizable today occurred in the final quarter of the century, when In 1876 it was converted into a hotel, which it remains. The pivotal transaction and rechristening followed swiftly, for In 1877 Addison Youngs sold his Bridgehampton farm and bought The American Hotel with his father-in-law and partner William Freeman. The new proprietors equipped it to a standard that distinguished it among its peers, for they bought the building and opened the American Hotel, offering such modern conveniences as electric lights and indoor plumbing. In keeping with the custom of the trade, As was the custom, the owner-proprietors and their families lived upstairs. The founder was an entrepreneur of considerable flair, as a singular exhibition venture attests: In 1888, entrepreneurs Addison Youngs, founder of the American Hotel, and Edwin Rogers, of Bridgehampton, embalmed a whale for exhibition. Their 100-foot "Prince of Whales" traveled the East Coast and west to Chicago. With a showman's cheerful cynicism, Youngs observed that The smell was a free advertisement.

VI. A Bath, a Bed, and a Scooter in the Hall: The Long Youngs Tenancy

For the better part of a century the hotel remained a family institution. The hotel remained in their family for almost a hundred years. During the lean decades of the Depression it served a practical and modest function, for During the factory days in the 1930s, the hotel was the only place in town where traveling salesmen could have a good dinner, a bath, and a clean room for the night for $2.25. The establishment preserved its respectability even amid the occasional intrusion, as a descendant recalled that Sometimes a drunk would sneak in through the back door, go upstairs and find a bed for a night—But on the whole it was a respectable place. Childhood memories lend the building a domestic warmth, for the family would spend their evenings in the front parlor, and one youthful resident confessed that he had a scooter and rode through the rooms after dinner, though his recollection of sleeping on the upper floor was rendered with a single word—when asked how it was, he answered, "Scary!". Through the dry years of Prohibition the hotel adapted pragmatically; The bar was removed and billiard tables set up in its place. Soft drinks were sold throughout the dry years, and when conditions permitted, William Youngs of the American Hotel was among those who received their two-year licenses. The long family chapter closed with a death, for When Jack's grandfather Will died in 1970 at 94, the family put the hotel up for sale, the last of a line who was the last owner.

VII. The Twenty-Four-Year-Old Visionary: Ted Conklin and the Modern Restoration

The hotel's rebirth came at the hands of a remarkably young and visionary buyer at a nadir in the village's fortunes. On a bitter day, the sellers met a potential buyer standing at the front door, a very young looking twenty-four year old Ted Conklin. His confident appraisal astonished the incumbents, for as he toured the faded rooms and the tin ceiling is kind of falling down, he declared, We can do this, we can do that, we can move this over there, prompting the family to conclude privately that this guy is crazy, Man. The skepticism was not unreasonable given the economic desolation of the moment, when It's 1972, it's a blue-collar town, there's no one around except the workers at the watchcase factory. The timing coincided with industrial collapse, for Three major factories closed between 1970 and 1972 throwing out of work over a thousand people, and across the village Main Street's businesses closed by the dozens. Store windows were boarded. Conklin's own account reveals both ambition and early struggle; his stated intention was to restore The American Hotel in the fashion of a community institution, yet he conceded that That business model failed because in those early years the local population had little money. Persistence prevailed, however, for Ted rolled up his sleeves and fixed up the entire hotel. Period wallpapers, antique furniture, an awardwinning wine cellar and the hotel quickly became the place to go for drinks and dinner. The institution's standing was formalized when, After Conklin's acquisition in 1972, The American Hotel was designated a village landmark - one of 13 - by the Sag Harbor Historic Preservation Commission in 1975. Its stewardship has remained singular and unbroken, for the hotel continues to be a destination at the center of Sag Harbor's Main Street and is still owned by the original Ted Conklin.

VIII. A Living Palimpsest: Endurance, Ornament, and Civic Memory

Through all its transformations the building has proven a survivor. It weathered even the great tempest of 1938, when Roofs were blown off the Post Office, hardware store, American Hotel, and grain company. It stands today as one of the trio of brick structures that were erected after the fire of 1845, and were spared in later fires, and it has come to serve almost as a visual synecdoche for the town itself, so much so that observers note the general look of downtown defined by the American Hotel and Municipal Building is largely unchanged. Its margins have long accommodated smaller enterprises, for to the left of the front porch of the hotel is a little shop that has housed many different businesses, among them a flower shop run by a proprietor who had a flower shop right in a part of the American Hotel on the left there. And it has entered the literary life of the village as surely as the civic, numbering among its devotees a distinguished playwright who was seen often heading on foot for the library or his favorite watering hole, The American Hotel. From colonial tavern to Tory garrison, from coffee house to cabinetmaker's phoenix, from family inn to landmarked destination, the structure at 49 Main Street—an elegant and professionally run establishment with an inimitable character—remains a living palimpsest of nearly three centuries of American life.
