The Present Structure: 842 Broadway, Union Square South (1998)

The present structure occupies a site long counted among the most conspicuous in Manhattan. It is situated on (14th Street between Fourth Avenue and Broadway), referenced in shorthand as (Union Square South (1998)), with its site given more precisely as (58-60 East 14th Street between Broadway and 4th Avenue, Union Square). The record is unambiguous on its age: (Was built in 1998.) This is echoed throughout the sources, which describe it as (Union Square South (1998)) and mark the moment (When Union Square South opened late in 1998.) Its celebrated public art followed close behind, for it was (Installed in 1999) and is formally dated as (Kristin Jones and Andrew Ginzel, Metronome, 1996-99, One Union Square South.)

It was (designed by Davis, Brody & Associates in association with Schuman, Lichtenstein, Claman & Efron), recorded elsewhere as (1999, Davis Brody Bond and Schuman Lichtenstein Claman & Efron.) The guiding vision ran along family lines, for (Davis, Brody's winning scheme, designed by Steven Davis, son of founding partner Lewis Davis, sought to merge the base with the tower.) The entertainment quarters were (designed by associate architects Schuman, Lichtenstein, Claman & Efron.) Direction came from a prominent developer, for the site was acquired by (the Related Companies) in 1995, and (the developer Stephen M. Ross went through over two hundred submissions before he chose the perplexing Metronome.)

Seen in the round, it is (a twenty-seven-story, 180-unit apartment building atop an eighty-five-foot-high mixed-use retail base that included a fourteen-screen theater.) Its footprint (occupied the highly visible 47,500-square-foot full-block site bounded by Fourth Avenue, Thirteenth Street, Broadway, and Fourteenth Street.) Accounts of capacity vary, with one noting (There are also 240 apartments here), and the building contained (fourteen theaters in this building) along with (the Virgin Megastore here) — a small city folded within a single block.

The structure declares its aesthetic allegiances in precise vocabulary. Its (Architecture Style_Postmodern.) The palette is rendered with clinical exactness: the (Base Color_Crystal - 213 213 213), and the (Façade Color_Sonic Silver - 166 166 166.) Its walls are of (Base Material_Granite Bulkheads, Limestone, Aluminum, Glass), and (Façade Material_Composite Panels.) The (Entrance Description_Storefront, Recessed), and the (Window Detail_Storefront.) Crowning it, the (Brickwork Style_Public Artwork: "Metronome"-Concentric Brick Circles, Bedrock), while the (Roofline Description_Flat, Parapet.)

The great artwork is the building's most arresting feature. The (Artwall, Kristin Jones and Andre and Andrew Genzel), is listed formally as (Metronome, Kristen Jones and Andrew Ginzel (1999).) The artists (had been chosen to embellish a 100-by-60-foot wall on the building's north facade, between the commercial base and the tower.) Its scale is extraordinary, for (The central component, measuring one hundred feet high and sixty feet wide, has an undulating brick wall with a dark circular void, surrounded by gold leaf overlay that dissipates across the panel into gold fragments.) In the artists' own language, (Jones and Ginzel proposed 'Metronome', confronting the street with a brick wall overlaid with an undulating wavelike pattern of concentric circles and a so-called eye, a four-foot-in-diameter opening that emitted clouds of steam.) That steam carried deliberate meaning, for (The steam, intended by the artists to recall the geothermal outpouring of Old Faithful, poured forward twice a day, at noon and at midnight, as part of the artists' intention to examine time itself.) Nor was it silent, since (Each emission was enhanced by the sounding of a long, tapered horn intended to echo the passage of time.) Adding a temporal dimension, (A digital clock on the left side of the wall augmented the frequent movements of the city by simultaneously counting and subtracting the hours of the day, with the converging digits in the middle absorbed by a blur of activity, intended to suggest the present.) Complementing this, (Below, a massive rock juts out through the brick wall, and to the left on the glass facade is a digital timepiece that counts the twenty-four hours of the day while simultaneously subtracting the time remaining in the day.) Balancing it, (To the right is the lunar timepiece, composed of a sphere set into a socket, which is synchronized to revolve with the phases of the moon.) Yet time has worn its machinery, for (then there is the steam, which used to puff out over 14th Street several times a day, but hasn't worked in several years.) The ambition was immense and costly, since (it's also one of the largest private commissions for public art in history), and (the three-million-dollar piece is meant to symbolize the intangibility of time.) That figure looms large, for (Though the theater was cheap, 'Metronome', at $3 million, was not.) Its spirit was celebratory, as (The complicated piece is an homage to the vibrant energy that is New York City.) For the patient passerby it (tells the time to the second in fifteen brightly lit numbers. Read the numbers from left to right to see the time as it ticks toward midnight; read from right to left to see the time count down to midnight.) Put another way, (It has fifteen digital numbers from left to right which count the minutes in a day and also show how much time remains in the day.)

Perhaps no observer captured its poetry more gently than the sociologist who paused beneath it: (Ahead of me, on 14th Street, I can see a large sundial and, to its left, a metronome. Sundials are the world's most ancient clocks, dating back perhaps to 3,500 BC. And the metronome is a modern-day way of measuring time. It has fifteen digital numbers from left to right which count the minutes in a day and also show how much time remains in the day. Watching the people hurrying by on this major thoroughfare, I feel that I'm looking at people in motion trying, in a way, to beat the clock while the images above chart their progress. An intriguing juxtaposition, I think.) And yet it remains an enigma to most, for (The giant wall piece that looms over the south side of Union Square is a mystery to many, with its swirled golden center, nonsensical-seeming counting numbers, and, of course, the now-ceased occasional emission of steam.)

It is at (14TH STREET BETWEEN FOURTH AVENUE AND BROADWAY IN MANHATTAN), served by (4, 5, 6, L N, Q, R, W to 14th Street-Union Square.) Its orientation was deliberate, for the wall faces (up the length of Park Avenue South.) Indeed, (The developers entered into an agreement with the Municipal Art Society to select an architectural firm that would maximize the site's most important attributes, its axial relationship to the vista south along Park Avenue from Grand Central Terminal and its prominent location at the edge of Union Square Park.) Out of this, (Related chose three firms to participate in a competition held in 1995: Davis, Brody & Associates, Robert A. M. Stern Architects, and Hardy Holzman Pfeiffer Associates, which later withdrew.) The artwork was chosen (In a separate competition organized by the Public Art Fund.)

Ambition and reception seldom agree. (But as built, its blocky mass was pretty banal, the cut reduced to virtual inconsequence, leaving the heavy aesthetic lifting to the work of the artists Kristin Jones and Andrew Ginzel.) The theater complex (irritated Herbert Muschamp, who found it "an appalling spot with all the charm of a high security prison.") He described how (the poor moviegoer rises through a bleakly Piranesian escalator core, past sheetrock walls painted a sullen grey) only to find, (on the landings, walls adorned with what look like tubes of spangled gift-wrap.) Muschamp (castigated the building as "the worst example yet of a formula that has helped to drain the life out of New York architecture in recent years: the use of public art to cover up for uninspired buildings.") He added that (Muschamp decried both the building's bulk and 'Metronome's' scale: the "artists' basic miscalculation was to assume that a large surface called for comparably big forms.") He concluded, ("Where's the architecture? . . . The answer is: There's no architecture here. This building has no idea what it is doing here. It has nothing fresh to say, not even an eloquent silence. It's just some space in a box with a Ieaky hole in it.") Some deferred, noting that (Judgment on the beauty of the building's "Artwall" facade, facing up the length of Park Avenue South, is probably best left to the eye of the beholder.) And the most candid response of all is that ("What the heck is that thing?" says everyone passing Metronome, ever.)

The Physical Fabric of the Block, Traced Through the Atlases (1853–1955)

Before the present tower rose, the block was a dense accretion of brick and wood, hotel and theater and store, recorded meticulously in more than a century of atlases and land books. To read them in sequence is to watch the block thicken, rise, and modernize decade by decade.

The earliest and most granular inventory comes from the mid-nineteenth century. (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'New York, Livery Stable, Union Place Hotel' as well as reference to 32 Structures; Brick or Stone dwellings second class (slate or metal roof not coped) with store under, Framed dwellings with store under and skylights lighting the floor only, Brick or Stone Structure with Brick or Stone Extension, Brick or Stone dwellings second class (slate or metal roof not coped) with store under, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with skylights lighting the top floor only... Brick or Stone Framed Store first class (slate or metal roof & coped).)

By mid-century's third decade the great theater had arrived, and the plan named the block's landmarks: (In the 1867 Dripps Plan, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'Gibsons Building, Wallacks Theatre, Union Hotel' as well as reference to 14 Structures; Building, Building, Building...)

By the 1880s, with Wallack's transformed into the Star, the atlas distinguished the durable from the flimsy — brick from wood: (In the 1885 Robinsons Atlas, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'Star Theatre, Union square Theatre, Morton house' as well as reference to 18 Structures; Brick building, Brick building... Wood building, Wood building, Wood building, Wood building, Wood building, Wood building.)

At the century's close the atlas recorded the rising story-heights of the block: (In the 1897 Bromleys Atlas, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'Star Theatre, Union Sq. Theatre, Morton Ho.' as well as reference to 25 Structures; 5 Story Brick building, 2 Story Brick building, 4 Story Brick building... 6 Story Brick building... 1 Story Brick building with Wood front, 1 Story Brick building with Wood front...)

By the early twentieth century the block bristled with lofts, a great clothier, a hotel, a vaudeville house — and saloons: (In the 1916 Atlas of the Borough of Manhattan, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'Lofts, Rogers Peet & Co., Hotel Churchill, Keith's Union Sq. Theatre' as well as reference to 12 Structures; 4 Story Brick Building with Store and 1 Story Brick Extension... 8 Story Brick Building with Store and Elevators and 1 Story Brick Extension, 2 Story Brick Building with Saloon, 2 Story Brick Building with Saloon, 2 Story Brick Building with Saloon, 3 Story Brick Building with Saloon, 3 Story Brick Building with Saloon and 1 Story Brick Extension.)

By 1930 the inventory had consolidated, and the arrival of the elevator and fireproof steel construction is plainly recorded: (In the 1930 Land Book of the Borough of Manhattan, on 842 Broadway, there is written 'Rogers Peet & Company, Acme Theatre' as well as reference to 7 Structures; Brick building, 2 Story Brick Building with Store, 2 Story Brick Building with Store, 2 Story Brick Building with Store, 3 Story Brick Building with Store, 3 Story Brick Building with Store and 1 Story Brick Extension, 8 Story Brick Building with Store and Elevator and Fire proof building (steel construction) and 1 Story Brick Extension.)

And the last pre-demolition inventory, at mid-century, preserved nearly the same configuration: (In the 1955 Manhattan Land Book, on 842 Broadway, there is reference to 7 structures; 2 story brick building with stores, 6 story brick building with stores, 3 story brick building with stores and a 1 story brick extension, 3 story brick building with stores, 2 story brick building with stores, 2 story brick building with stores, 8 story brick building with stores and elevators and fireproof building (steel construction) and a 1 story brick extension.)

The Predecessors on the Site

Wallack's Theatre / The Star Theatre

Chief among the ghosts is the playhouse that gave the corner its fame. (If a single theater can be credited with linking Broadway with theatrical excellence, it would undoubtedly be Wallack's Theatre on the northeast corner of Broadway and Thirteenth Street. Built in 1861 as the home for Lester Wallack's stock company, it introduced true ensemble playing to New York through classical and contemporary comedies.) Its life divided in two, for (When Wallack's troupe moved to a new theater in 1881, the house began a second career as the Star Theatre. On its Stage appeared Sarah Bernhardt, Edwin Booth, Sir Henry Irving, Ellen Terry, and Modjeska, and it remained the city's most prestigious theater until its demolition in 1901.)

Its origins followed fashion northward: (At the beginning of the decade Wallack moved his company from Broadway and Broome Street, in the heart of the theatrical district, to a new and Splendid home uptown. The new Wallack's Theater was built on the northeast corner of Broadway and 13th Street, on land rented from William Backhouse Astor. It was one of the largest playhouses in the city, and in its architecture and furnishings, its handsome lobbies and lounges, represented the last word in elegance.) Its economy of admission was carefully graded, for (Orchestra stalls sold for one dollar; for fifty cents you could sit in the parquet, or pit; gallery gods spent only twenty-five cents for seats in the family circle. Private boxes seating seven were on sale at seven dollars, but only parvenus occupied them.) So exclusive was its clientele that (To play at Wallack's was the best of all passports to fame. First nights there brought out the most distinguished audience to be found in New York: the aristocracy of wealth and fashion, the leading celebrities of the stage and opera, the most beautiful 'demi-mondaines', the authors, artists and journalists of whom everyone was talking. You attended Wallack's, like the opera, as much to be seen as to see.)

The literal plan of the house, in its Star years, is preserved: (Star Theatre, BROADWAY and 13th STREET. / Image Caption: Stage, Box A, Orchestra, Box B, Box C, Balcony, Box D.)

Architectural histories confirm its pedigree and afterlife: (Lester Wallack's first theater, at 485 Broadway, just south of Broome Street, opened in 1858. In 1861 he commissioned from Thomas R. Jackson a second theater, at 844 Broadway, at Thirteenth Street, and for twenty years this would be the most famous theater in the United States. By 1880, however, the theater was losing out to new uptown competitors such as the Booth, so Wallack redecorated the interior. Sensing that something more drastic was required, Wallack closed the theater in 1881 and hired George A. Freeman Jr. to design a new playhouse... The old Wallack Theater was renamed the Star and continued under other management.) It witnessed history as well as artistry, for it saw (the New York debut of actor John Wilkes Booth as Richard III. Booth came from a family of actors, but his performances from March 17 through April 5, 1862, received less-than-stellar reviews. His mediocre acting ability was always compared to that of his brother Edwin, widely considered the greatest Shakespearean actor in America.)

Its end was as historic as its heyday, for the old house (was replaced in 1902 by the present structure built by Rogers Peet & Co., the men's clothiers. Having made four decades of theatrical history, the theater made movie history in 1901 when its demolition became the subject of an early documentary film.) One later account added that (The card depicts the January 2, 1902, blaze that damaged the stores and basement but spared the theater... The Star was known as Wallick's when the area, pictured on the 13th Street block, was the theatre district.)

The Union Square Theatre

A stone's throw away stood another celebrated house, whose life was longer still. (The Union Square Theater, at 58 East Fourteenth Street, opened on September 11, 1871. It was not a new building but an alteration within the shell of what had been the Union Place Hotel, facing Union Square between Broadway and Fourth Avenue.) Like so many houses of its era, (typical of most New York theaters, it was a largely hidden asset, concealed behind the dour facade of the old hotel.) But behind that dour front lay glamour, for it was (distinguished by what the 'New York Daily Tribune' described as "a cheerful and commodious entrance. . . . The name of the new theater appears, in letters of fire, above the portal.") Inside, the Tribune noted, ("The vestibule stretches from beneath spacious porticos, and is brilliantly frescoed, in green and red, and brilliantly lighted.") Comfort was sacrificed to capacity, for (In order to pack 1,200 seats into a comparatively small area, the auditorium was built very high), so much so that the Herald's correspondent complained that from the parquet ("the decoration of the dome and even those over the proscenium are scarcely visible.") Of the artistry itself the Herald was faintly damning: ("Art, too, has been called in to aid in making the Union Theatre a fit temple for the muses. We cannot say that the art is of a very high quality, but in view of the very low state of decorative painting in America, it is not, perhaps, fair to be over-fastidious.")

Fire struck, for (A fire in February 1888 destroyed the roof and parts of the auditorium, and the theater reopened in 1889, rebuilt to designs reputedly by Leopold Eidlitz and John E. Terhune, although some thought the design was by Charles P. Palmer, the manager of the property.) The rebuilt interior was resplendent, for (The interior of the rebuilt theater was painted in gold and ivory and the proscenium arches contained a large medallion incorporating a painting of Shakespeare.) A terse catalogue records the whole arc of its structural life: (Erected in centre of Union Place Hotel; opened 1871... destroyed by fire 1888... rebuilt and became Keith's Union Square Theatre 1893.)

Its stage saw remarkable milestones. (Here in 1876 occurred the New York stage debut of matinee idol James O'Neill, whose declining years were to be dramatized by his playwright son Eugene in 'Long Day's Journey into Night'. This theater was also the setting on August 20, 1883, of the first performance anywhere of a play by Oscar Wilde—though 'Vera, or the Nihilist', a premiere that the author attended, proved a disaster and barely finished out the week. Here George M. Cohan made his debut as a solo performer, soon after the Union Square began a vaudeville policy in 1893.) In time the moving image supplanted the actor, for (Two years later the Union Square became one of the earliest established theaters to project motion pictures. Initially a novelty on the vaudeville bill, motion pictures completely replaced live acts at this theater in 1908. Since 1936, the theater has been closed.) Its fate was near-total erasure, for (the historic Union Square Theater (1871, rebuilt, John Terhune and Leopold Eidlitz, 1889), 58 East Fourteenth Street, which had long been obscured behind a huge McDonald's sign) was cleared to make way for the present tower.

The Union Place Hotel / Morton House / Hotel Churchill

Both theaters were, in their beginnings, entangled with a single great hostelry. (On the southeast corner of Broadway and Fourteenth Street is the Hotel Churchill, formerly the Morton House, and originally the Union Place Hotel, established in 1850.) Its shifting identities are traced in the record: (The tall building in the next block east was the Union Place Hotel, leased at this time to John C. Wheeler. Sheridan Shook leased it in 1871, and called it the Maison Doree. In 1881 it became known as the Morton House. This building, as well as the one on the south-east corner of Broadway and 14th Street, was owned by Cortlandt Palmer.) Its founding is fixed among a roster of that year's new structures, for the record notes (the Union Place Hotel, at 860 Broadway), and further that (For a history of the Union Place Hotel (known as the Morton House after 1868), at the south-east corner of Broadway and 14th St., see the N. Y. Times, Je 12, 1921.) Its long service is summarized thus: (Morton House, originated around 1850 as Union Place Hotel, expanded several times and was named for the Morton Brothers, operators from 1868 to the 1880s. It was demolished around 1926.) In the end only bare ground remained, marking the (Site of Morton House Hotel (also known as the Hotel Churchill, Union Place Hotel).)

The Rogers Peet Building at 842 Broadway

Finally, rising upon the very ground where Wallack's had stood, came the commercial building that succeeded it. Known to many as (Also known as the Village Voice offices), it is properly the (ROGERS, PEET BUILDING, No. 842), and its details are these: (1902, by Clinton & Russell. [8 stories] The red-brick facade is divided into bays topped by large segmental arches. There are copper dormers on the mansard roof. Site of Wallack's Theater (also known as the Germania Theater, Star), 1860-61, by Thomas R. Jackson, remodeled 1883 and 1889.) Old atlases capture the layered commerce of its heyday, recording at 842 Broadway both ('Rogers Peet & Company, Acme Theatre') and, earlier, ('Lofts, Rogers Peet & Co., Hotel Churchill, Keith's Union Sq. Theatre.')

Thus does a single corner hold the memory of livery stables and first-class brick dwellings, of ensemble drama and vaudeville, of grand hotels and men's clothiers, of the debut of John Wilkes Booth and the first American play of Oscar Wilde — its story written across a century of atlases in ever-rising story-heights, in the slow triumph of brick over wood and steel over brick, and now overlooked by a tower of crystal and silver, and by a great golden eye that counts, ceaselessly and to the second, the passing of the time in which all these things happened and were lost.
