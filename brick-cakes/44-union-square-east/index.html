Before the Wigwam: The Vacant Plot and the First Dwellings

Long before this corner acquired its notoriety, it lay all but untouched. The earliest surviving record reveals a parcel of ground still awaiting its destiny, for (In the 1815 Blue Book, on 44 Union Square East, there is reference to 0 structures.) Within a generation, gracious residences had taken root upon the land, as (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 44 Union Square East, there is reference to 4 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension.) By the years following the Civil War, a quartet of buildings crowded the site, for (In the 1867 Dripps Plan, on 44 Union Square East, there is reference to 4 structures; Building, Building, Building, Building.)

The Westmoreland and the Age of Hotels

As the district matured into a fashionable quarter, a hotel came to dominate the corner. (In the 1885 Robinsons Atlas, on 44 Union Square East, there is written 'West Moreland Hotel' as well as reference to 2 Structures; Brick building, Brick building.) This hostelry endured through the century's twilight, when (In the 1897 Bromleys Atlas, on 44 Union Square East, there is written 'West Moreland Hotel' as well as reference to 3 Structures; 5 Story Brick building, 7 Story Brick building, 3 Story Brick building with Basement.) Its name persisted, though in slightly altered form, into the new century, for (In the 1916 Atlas of the Borough of Manhattan, on 44 Union Square East, there is written 'Westmoreland' as well as reference to 3 Structures; 5 Story Brick building with Store, 7 Story Brick building with Store and Elevator, 3 Story Brick building with Basement and Store.) A neighboring establishment met its end during these same years, its passing dutifully noted in the record: (The Clarendon Hotel, at the south-east corner of 17th St. and Fourth Ave., has been closed.—N. Y. Times, O 14, 1898.)

The Arrival of Tammany Hall

The edifice destined to define this corner arose out of the restless wanderings of a formidable political dynasty. Long entrenched elsewhere in the city, the machine drifted northward across the decades, for (Tammany Hall's first headquarters were located downtown near city hall; they moved into a building on this site in 1867. Tammany remained here until 1928, when it sold the building to Consolidated Gas and moved to its third and final location, at Street (the present-day New York Film Academy building), where it only lasted a couple of years before finally going out of business. Tammany Hall was a notoriously corrupt political organization with very close ties to the criminal underworld of the Lower East Side.) The blueprint for its final home crystallized at the close of the 1920s, when (Late in 1927, the Society of Tammany sold its headquarters on East 14th Street and announced the construction of a new Tammany Hall. Plans for the new building were made public in January 1928.) As another chronicler recounts the migration, (Tammany Hall, whose "Wigwam" had stood for decades in 14th Street and whose Democratic leaders still ruled New York, moved in 1928 into a new marble building at 100 East 17th Street, comer of Fourth Avenue.)

The new hall rose to completion at the very summit of the organization's power and prestige. (Tammany Hall reached its zenith in 1928. Smith was a powerful and widely respected governor; Walker was an extraordinarily popular mayor, and George W. Olvany, a college graduate, was the boss. A new building was completed in 1929 in Union Square at 17th Street, and during this period even refromers had few Criticisms of Tammany Hall.) More tersely, one record simply affirms that it (Was built in 1929.)

The Building Itself: Architecture and Symbolism

The commission fell to distinguished architects, and the chosen idiom bore a meaning far weightier than mere ornament. (On the southeast corner of East 17th Street and Union Square East, at 100-102 East 17th Street, stands the second of the district's institutional buildings. Tammany Hall, once the headquarters of New York City's Democratic Earn political machine.) The intentions animating the design were made plain from the outset, for (The building, designed by Thompson, Holmes & Converse and Charles B. Meyers, was to be a Colonial Revival structure built of red Harvard brick with granite trim (limestone was later substituted). The Real Estate Record described the design as "a dignified architectural treatment, one of the chief motifs of which arc the severe Colonial columns in the centers of the Union Square and the Seventeenth-street facades which recall the days of early American architecture.')

The symbolic calculation behind the style was anything but incidental, for (The Colonial Revival was undoubtedly chosen not only because it was reminiscent of the architecture of the early republic, bur also because it symbolized the government established during that period. The Society of Tammany was attempting to associate itself with the ideals of early American democracy: in fact, the Union Square facade bears a striking resemblance to the original Federal Hall, where George Washington took the oath of office as the first President of the United States. By choosing the Colonial Revival style, with all that it symbolized, Tammany may have been attempting to create the image of a truly democratic force to counter the accusations that it was a corrupt political machine.)

The precise vocabulary of its architecture has been catalogued with meticulous exactitude: (_neo-Georgian, Contemporary, _Rusticated Limestone, <Façade Material>_Clinker Brick, Limestone, Terra Cotta, _Molded Arched Surround, Gothic Arched Transom, _2nd fl. Balcony, Flat Arch Brick Lintels, Stone Keystones, Projecting Sills, Window Panes: [8/8], Window Panes: [12/12], _Doric Pilasters, Doric Columns, Pedimented Portico, Double Height Pilasters, Stone Console Brackets, Quoins, Sculptural Relief Tablet, Rondels, _Doric Entablature, Garlands, Roundel, Balustrade, Hipped Roof, Brick/ Stone Parapet, Construction: Hipped Roof Changing to Shell Dome.)

An official designation record contemplates the same façade with comparable admiration: (This distinguished neo-Georgian facade, with its rusticated stone base, double-height pilasters, and sculptural reliefs in terra cotta and limestone, was inspired by the original Federal Hall (demolished 1812) on Wall Street, where the first President of the United States, George Washington, took his oath of office. Clad with specially molded and oversized brick laid in Flemish bond, other neo-Georgian elements of the facade, such as the blind arches that frame the multilight sash windows, the fanlight transoms, wrought iron balconies, and semicircular attic window, deliberately evoke the dignity of early American architecture.) A later guide reiterates the same illustrious lineage: (The Union Square facade of the building including the first-floor balcony under the portico was deliberately modelled on that of New York's original Federal Hall on Wall Street (demolished 1812) where Washington was inaugurated as our nation's first President.)

The finished structure was heralded in its own moment, as one caption of the day proclaims: (Image Caption: The new Tammany Hall, facing Union Square at 17th Street and 4th Avenue. Built in 1929.) The land records of the era duly registered its arrival, for (In the 1930 Land Book of the Borough of Manhattan, on 44 Union Square East, there is written 'Tammy Hall' as well as reference to 1 Structure; 4 Story Brick building with Store Elevator and Fire proof building (steel construction).)

One emblematic flourish still keeps its silent vigil over the park, for (another liberty cap—across the street in the pediment of the old Tammany meeting place at 100 East Seventeenth Street—is eminently visible as it oversees the park and the Charles F. Murphy Monument.) And even through the building's many transformations, that ancient token of freedom has endured, since (the exterior, including the Liberty Cap on the pediment facing Union Square, long a symbol of the society, has been preserved.)

The Character of Tammany Hall

The society that raised this hall was among the most fiercely polarizing institutions ever to grace American public life. (TAMMANY HALL!!—revered by its constituents; feared by neutrals; hated by its opponents! The New York Tammany Society was founded about 1786, and organized under a constitution in 1789 as a charitable and patriotic order. It has gradually developed into one of the most powerful political influences in the history of our nation.) Its very name reached back into an older, mythic past, drawn as it was from (its name from a sachem of the Delaware Indians (Tammany—one of the chiefs of the Algonquins, living on the banks of the Delaware River, who for years held undisputed sway over Eastern America) this great society, from its very organization became a dominating factor in the councils of the Democratic Party and has commanded such a power in local, state, and even national politics, that the name ''Tammany" has become known world over.)

The fervor it inspired ran to the very extremes of human sentiment, for (Tammany Hall is the shrine at which hundreds of thousands of loyal democrats worship, and point with pride to its achievements. On the other hand, its opponents damn Tammany Hall as the hellish headquarters for graft, corruption, and misused power! Has any institution ever organized outside of fanatical religious circles, been so beloved or so hated; so lauded or so execrated; so eulogized or so condemned!) Its dominion over the city was near-absolute, for (New York politics arc so intimately identified with Tammany Hall it is said that the Democratic Party in New York City is so completely controlled by this powerful organization that no nominee has even a fighting chance for victory unless endorsed by Tammany.)

The organization's genesis and its contested legacy are elsewhere distilled with clarity: (Tammany Hall, now a byword for municipal corruption, once dominated the City's political arena. An outgrowth of the eighteenth-century Tammany Society, Tammany Hall quickly became affiliated with the newly formed Democratic Party, promoting public education, universal suffrage for white males, and helping to abolish debtors' prisons; as well as providing support for newly arrived Irish, French, and German immigrants. By the mid-nineteenth century, Tammany was firmly in charge of the municipal government, and under the leadership of the now infamous "Boss" William Tweed.) It was precisely a bid at reinvention and rehabilitation that gave rise to this very building, since (His fall, and the emergence of a "New Tammany" in the 1920s, refashioned as a "good government" organization, has been identified by some as the impetus for the construction of this New Tammany Hall, the only surviving headquarters building of Tammany.) Yet another account traces the fellowship back to its revolutionary roots, noting that (The club was founded in 1788 as an outgrowth of the patriotic Sons of Liberty and called The Society of St. Tammany or Columbian Order.)

The Fall and the Sale

The machine's descent followed hard upon the completion of its grandest monument. (But the organizations fortunes soon changed. Investigations of civic corruption by Samuel Seabury led to Walker's resignation in 1932.) The reverberations of scandal reshaped the whole political landscape, for (John F. Curry, Olvany's successor, sought to block Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidential nomination, allowing Flynn, no longer an ally of Tammany Hall, to become the strongest link between the Democratic White House and the city. In 1933 Mayor John P. O'Brien, the incumbent and a loyalist chosen by Curry, finished last in a three-way mayoral election won by Fiorello H. La Guardia, who led a coalition opposed to Tammany Hall that remained in place for 12 years.) The essence of that undoing is captured succinctly in the observation that (By the early 1930s the extent of the society's graft and corruption was widely recognized, and Tammany Mayor James J. Walker was forced to resign in 1932.)

There followed the loss of the building itself, a downfall as material as it was symbolic, for (Unable to meet mortgage payments, the sachems sold their building to Local 91 of the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union in 1943. By the time the Democrats recaptured the mayoralty in 1945, Tammany Hall had virtually ceased to exist, although politicians bred in the organization continued to flourish into the 1950s and beyond.) By mid-century, the ledgers themselves bore witness to the transformation, for (In the 1955 Manhattan Land Book, on 44 Union Square East, there is reference to 1 Structure; 4 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete building with Elevator and Fire proof building (steel construction).)

From Union Hall to Theatre and Film Academy

The building's later chapters interwove the causes of organized labor with the enchantments of the stage. Reflecting upon this melancholy yet fitting reinvention, one observer described how (the trim neo-Federal-style brick-and-limestone building, No. 100 East 17th Street (Thompson, Holmes & Converse and Charles B. Meyers, 1929), was the "final resting place" of Tammany Hall, the Democratic political machine that held the city in a corrupt stranglehold for almost two centuries.) A preservationist mused upon its abiding significance, observing that ("the building is such a great example of Colonial Revival, of a time when a group tried to burnish its crooked reputation by linking itself to early American ideals," adding, "the history of Tammany Hall, shabby as it is, is also a big part of our past, and this building is the sole physical remnant of that.") There even arose a mischievous proposal for its future, for (While awaiting the city's decision on landmark designation, it was suggested that the Tammany Hall building be converted into a "Museum of Political Corruption"; however, one wag observed that "it just wouldn't be large enough!")

The building passed through many hands and served many masters across the decades that followed. (In 1929, when the building was completed, Tammany had again fallen out of favor, and by 1943, the Society sold the building to Local 91 of the International Ladies Garment Workers Union, which occupied it until 1984. That year, the Local entered into a lease agreement with the Roundabout Theatre, which renovated and occupied the building's auditorium until 1991, when it moved to 45th Street. Local 91 subsequently leased the auditorium to producer Raymond L. Gaspard, and then to the owners of Minetta Lane Theatre. In 1994, the Local began leasing portions of the building not occupied by the theater to the New York Film Academy; in 2001, the entire building was sold to Liberty Theatres and four years later the air rights were conveyed to US Development LLC.) A parallel account renders its theatrical rebirth in gentler strokes: (By 1943, unable to meet its mortgage payments, Tammany sold the building to Local 91 of the International Ladies Garment Workers Union. In 2001, they, in turn, sold it to Liberty Theaters, who remodeled the union's former meeting hall—once called the Roosevelt Auditorium—into the present Union Square Theater. The building is also home to the New York Film Academy, a noted school for filmmakers.) A further summary confirms this arc of stewardship, noting that (A decade later the society sold its meeting hall to the International Ladies Garment Workers Union, and it became a major center for union activity, in 1984 the Roundabout Theater Company took over for a decade before heading uptown. The building is now being converted to office and retail use.) Its tenure under the union's banner was recorded thus: (In recent years it has housed the International Ladies Garment Workers Union Local 99-105, and its 499-seat auditorium has been used by the Roundabout Theater Company, a group with 20,000 subscribers.)

The Roundabout Theatre Company

The building's theatrical epoch commenced with an audacious act of conversion. Situated at (100 East 17th Street at Park Avenue South, Box Office: 420-1883, Seating: 499 (Orchestra 358; Mezzanine, 141), Handicapped: Semi-accessible), the company forged a reputation of considerable distinction, for (Since its founding in 1965 Artistic Director Gene Feist has built the Roundabout into one of the major nonprofit theatrical institutions in the country. A number of well-known actors got their New York start here, including Amanda Plummer, David Dukes, B. D. Wong, Kate Burton, Danny Glover, and Jerry Zaks.) The reimagining of the space itself was a remarkable feat of transformation, since (In 1984 the Roundabout converted a 1,000-seat auditorium in the old Tammany Hall into an intimate 500-seat theatre.)

The Union Square Theatre

Under its subsequent name, the venue flourished as a bustling Off Broadway house. Located plainly at (100 East 17th Street, New York, NY 10003), it accrued a spectral folklore all its own, for (The present Union Square Theatre, according to a New York Times article, was reportedly haunted by a phantom that lived in a crawl space and had eyes like a cal that glowed in the dark. Windows and a chandelier once started making a racket on their own, and did not quit until the maintenance man demanded that whoever - or whatever - knock it off so he could get some sleep. And the sounds stopped.) As a theatre, it was esteemed as (A large Off Broadway house that has played host to such shows as Wit, The Laramie Project, BatBoy, and others. Call one to three weeks in advance.) Its programming in the mid-1990s boasted productions of genuine renown, among them: (Vita and Virginia: Vanessa Redgrave and Eileen Atkins star in, and Zoe Caldwell directs, this play based on the letters of Sackville West and Woolf. Union Square Theatre. 100 E 17th, 307-4100, through 2/26.) Its benefit evenings, too, summoned a glittering assembly of talent: (State of Siege, Sun: Vanessa Redgrave and Eileen Atkins host an evening of words and music to benefit the children of Sarajevo, 7. Also appearing will be Glenn Close, Stockard Channing, Mira Furlan, John Turturro, Christopher Reeve, Harvey Keitel, Simon Shaheen, Joseph Brodsky, Paul Auster, Ariel Dorfman, Susan Sontag, Tony Kushner, and Wendy Wasserstein. Union Square Theatre, 100 E 17th, 505-0700. $) The hall likewise lent itself to purposes of oratory, for it was here that (Bly delivered a series of lectures here about her record-breaking trip around the globe.)

The New York Film Academy

The film school emerged as one of the building's most defining modern occupants, proclaiming its presence from this address with characteristic boldness: (LEARN FILM MAKING), offering (SUMMER WORKSHOPS AT PRINCETON UNIVERSITY & NEW YORK FILM ACADEMY and) inviting aspirants to (DIRECT YOUR OWN SHORT FILMS IN OUR HANDS-ON INTENSIVE WORKSHOPS USING 16MM ARRIFLEX CAMERAS. LEARN DIRECTING, STORY, CAMERA, SOUND AND EDITING IN A TOTAL IMMERSION COURSE DESIGNED AND TAUGHT BY AWARD WINNING INSTRUCTORS.) Its address was fixed with precision, for the academy stood at (100 EAST 17TH STREET NYC 10003 TEL: 212 674-4300 FAX: 212 477-1414 FORMERLY LOCATED AT TRIBECA FILM CENTER), and its identity was distilled into a single caption: (Image Caption: NEW YORK FILM ACADEMY.)

The Surrounding Square and Neighborhood

The building presides over a park steeped in history. (Union Square was originally called Union Place in 1811. During the late nineteenth century, 14th Street was the midway point for Ladies' Mile, a walkway of swanky shops that extended from Broadway and 8th to 23rd Street.) In time, the square became a crucible of dissent, for (In the years prior to World War I, Union Square evolved into a forum for anarchists, socialists, Wobblies, and Communists, where they took to soapboxes to espouse their political leanings.) Its radical vocation deepened amid the privations of the Depression, when (The most well-known confrontation was a Depression-era workers protest of 35,000 that led to public outcry after police attacked one hundred of the protestors. Union Square was thereafter the soul of militant political movements in the City.) It remained, above all, a sanctuary of liberty, for (It is proper that the Independence Flagstaff stands at the center of Union Square, a park that has meant independence and freedom of speech for generations. There, especially during the 1930s, people came to speak their minds without fear of assault or arrest.)

Close at hand rose other monuments to commercial ambition, chief among them the (EVERETT BLDG.) at (45 Union Square), described in its particulars as (N. W. Cor. 16th St. & 4th Ave. Cost ...$2,500,000, 252 ft. high..............18 Floors, Rent Area... 195.000 sq. ft. Housing ...2300, Completed…1900, Owner... Everett Investing Co., Arch... Starrett & Van Vleck, Bldr...Gen. A. Fuller Co. Agent... H. Van R. Forest.) The immediate blocks were rich, too, in literary association, for (the last home of Jeannette L. Gilder, long editor of the Critic and arresting, if sometimes amusing, figure in the literary life of the town a generation ago, was at 100 East Seventeenth Street.) That same journalist is elsewhere recorded with plainer economy as (Gilder, Jeannette Leonard: born Flushing New York, October 3rd, 1849; Journalist.)
