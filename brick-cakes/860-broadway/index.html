The Land Before the Building

Long before any structure rose on this corner, the land belonged to the great colonial estates of Manhattan. This was a district shaped by the farms of old New York, where (Other farms as far as Twenty-third Street on the west side belonged to Thomas Burling, John Cowman, Isaac Clason, Sir Peter Warren, Isaac Varian, and Christian Milderberger. On the east side, were the two farms of Cornelius Williams and John Watts. At the corner of the present Seventeenth Street and the Bloomingdale Road was a square acre of ground belonging to the Manhattan Bank, acquired so it is supposed, as a sort of refuge for conducting business in case of being driven from the city by the yellow fever.)

Among these estates was one of singular importance. (The Warren farm is one of the most interesting estates on Manhattan Island. It includes the Indian village of Sapokanican; the Dutch village of Northwyck, and the English settlement of Greenwich, which did not include all of the "Greenwich village" of modern New York.)

The east side of Broadway in this stretch was dominated by grand landholdings: (On the east side of Broadway, from Fourteenth Street to Nineteenth Street, and covering some eight or ten blocks, including part of Union Square, was the estate of Tiebout Williams, next adjoining which was the Krom-Messie property, running back to near the Second Avenue, in about the centre of which Grammercy is now situated. Adjoining this estate and extending around the Post Road was the property of John Watts, a leading citizen of New York.)

The curious square acre at the corner belonged to a bank whose presence puzzled even contemporaries. (A large building on the East side of Broadway, between Seventeenth and Eighteenth streets, was known as the Manhattan Bank1, and so designated in a map of a later day; but why so termed I have forgotten. The weather at this time was so intensely cold that the head and all harbors of Long Island Sound were closed by ice.) This branch was a genuinely early structure — (Bank of the Manhattan Company (branch). 846-2 Site: E. side Broadway, bet. 17th and 18th Sts. Erected shortly after 1806) — and it did not survive the mid-century, being (Demolished bet. 1847 and 1849.)

In these earliest years, the address itself held nothing at all: in the 1815 record, (on 860 Broadway, there is reference to 0 structures.)

The Hotel Era

By mid-century the district had blossomed into a fashionable quarter of grand hotels, and 860 Broadway became one of the earliest. (In 1850 Union Place Hotel Occupied No. 860 Broadway.)

The neighborhood's transformation was rapid and celebrated: (Union Square had never been an exclusively residential location, like Fifth Avenue or Gramercy Park. In 1851 the Daily Tribune described Union Square as the favored location in the city for sumptuous family hotels. The Union Place Hotel, completed in 1850, stood at 860 Broadway, at the southeast comer of Fourteenth Street.)

It stood among a distinguished company of establishments — (The finest hotels dating from this building boom were the American Hotel at Broadway and Grand Street; the Union Place at 860 Broadway, at Fourteenth Street) — and it would eventually take on a second identity, for there survives (a history of the Union Place Hotel (known as the Morton House after 1868), at the south-east corner of Broadway and 14th St.)

The maps of the period reflect this dense residential-commercial mix. In 1853, (on 860 Broadway, there is reference to 3 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped), Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped), Brick or Stone Store first class.) By 1867, the record simply notes (3 structures; Building, Building, Building.)

A stroll through the neighborhood in this era captured its restless glamour: (The Everett occupies the opposite corner of the square, and now we are back into Broadway again at the corner of Seventeenth street. From this point to Thirty-fourth street, can be seen a succession of stores and hotels that tell at once the story of our grandeur and our folly, and which, when the reaction against fast living that many sensible people are looking for, comes, will loom up as monuments of faded splendor.)

Electricity and Antiques

As the Gilded Age dawned, the address became a site of innovation. (Electricity came to Broadway in 1880, when the Brush Electric Light Company opened offices at 860 Broadway, one block north of Union Square. Charles Francis Brush, thirty-one, who wore the era's requisite handlebar mustache and an optimistic, steadfast expression, was an engineer and inventor of enormous intelligence, with a Barnum-like flare for theatrical self-promotion. Among Brush's patents were a more efficient electrical generator and an improved electric-arc lamp.)

The corner also became a home for fine furnishings and antiques. Under the heading of (ANTIQUITIES), the directories listed (Sypher & Co., 860 and 1351 Broadway), and in the shopping guides again (Sypher & Co., 860 Broadway.) The address also served the storage trade, listing under (STORAGE) the name (Rogers, John, 860 Broadway.) By 1885 the atlas confirmed the occupant, showing ('Sypher & Co. Antique Furniture' and 1 Structure; Brick building.)

The Parish Building Rises

Then came the structure that stands today. (Was built in 1884.)

Its full pedigree is rich: (PARISH BUILDING, No. 860 (Also known as the Butler Building, Lienau Building, Thonet Brothers Depot. Andy Warhol's Factory). 1883-84, by Detlef Lienau; remodeled 1925, by F. H, Dewey & Company (6 stories), The vigorous original building was considerably watered down to its current yellow brick version Site of Henry Parish house (also known as the Union League Club).)

The building's origins lay in commerce and family legacy: (When business picked up in the 1880s, the Parish family's namesake building (1883-84), at 860 Broadway, on the northeast comer of Seventeenth Street, was one of the more interesting new designs, a late work of Detlef Lienau. The Parish Building was developed by descendants of Henry' Parish, the dry-goods merchant, whose Italianate brownstone mansion (R. G. Hatfield, 1847-48) previously occupied the site and was home to the Union.)

Not everyone admired it. (Although Madison Square and the east side of Union Square in the early 1880s were still unadorned by tall office and manufacturing buildings, the stretch of Ladies Mile along Broadway had changed. The Real Estate Record drew attention to the northeast corner of Seventeenth Street and Broadway (860 Broadway) where a building stood of such poor architectural composition that it gave the appearance of two buildings, one set on top of the other. As the building was conspicuously located, the Real Estate Record found its commonplace design by Detlef Lienau regrettable.)

The successive layers of commercial occupancy defined the corner for decades, and a later view recorded that (Many commercial successors to the Parish Mansion, including the new Union Place Hotel would continue to lake over this corner until 1893 when lots were merged and the extant six story commercial structure designed by Detlef Lienau was erected.)

Its physical character was catalogued in painstaking architectural detail — a (Late-19th-century-Commercial) style, built of (Buff Brick, Stone, Terra Cotta), with a (Chamfered, Recessed, Iron Surround) entrance, (Spiraled Iron Enframements), a (Flemish Bond) brickwork, and a roofline crowned by a (Projecting Cornice, Modillions, Rosette Frieze, Dentils, Scalloped Parapet.)

A Corner of Commerce

By 1897 the atlas showed the block filled with (3 structures; 6 Story Brick building, 6 Story Brick building, 6 Story Brick building.)

A vivid streetscape survives from the turn of the century, when the corner teemed with businesses: (860 to 872 Broadway, Between 17th and 18th Streets. Thonet Bros., Bentwood Furniture-Peter M. Reilly, Cigars-New York Barber Co.-Conrad's Lunch Room-Morse Bros., Men's Wear-Paul L Bryant, Dyeing and Cleaning -Old Staten Island Dyeing And Cleaning Co. - Plymouth Raincoat Co.)

Through the twentieth century the building took on new commercial tenants and identities. In 1916 it was recorded with the notation ('Lofts, Parish Bldg. Lofts') and described as (2 Structures; 6 Story Brick building with Store and Elevator and 1 Story Brick Extension.) By 1930 the name over the door had changed, reading ('Butler Bros') with reference to (1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building with Store and Elevator,) and by 1955 it still bore ('Butler Bros.') as (1 Structure; 6 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete building with Store and Elevators and 1 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete Extension.)

Andy Warhol's Factory

The building's most legendary chapter began in the 1970s. The listing is simple and iconic: (ANDY WARHOL STUDIO, 860 Broadway In 1974. Warhol moves to a nearby studio that came to be known as "the office." He stayed here until 1984.)

The move itself was pure Warhol: (By 1974, Warhol had run out of space at his Union Square studio called The Factory and, in August of that year, he moved across the street to this six-story brick building. The space was located on the third floor and included over 12,000 square feet of offices, storage space, and studios. Always economical, Warhol made it a condition that all visitors to The Factory during the move had to carry at least one item across the street to the new building.)

The space had a storied past of its own: (The third floor of this building, once the offices of S&H Green Stamps in the twenties, was the setting for Warhol's third Factory from 1974-1984. Here, Andy Warhol Enterprises, initially set up in 1957 for tax purposes, took off. The new Union Square space was just down the block from the former one and had a back elevator, an added level of security to prevent another Valerie Solanas-type incident from taking place.)

Its interiors reflected Warhol's new grandeur: (The new offices occupied the entire third floor of the building at 860 Broadway. Decades earlier, it had been home to S&H Green Stamps, whose products had been the subject of some of Warhol's earliest Pop Art. Their vintage offices came with a grand, wood-paneled boardroom, shipped over from England and perfectly suited to Warhol's newly conservative image. When Fremont and Hughes first saw the space, the boardroom was surrounded by yawning empty spaces interrupted only by slender cast-iron columns; they must once have been filled with typists and clerks.)

Here, one of Warhol's most enduring projects was born, (One of the first was accidental: Warhol's Time Capsules. Warhol took standard packing boxes left over from the move from 33 Union Square a block away, and began to fill them with hundreds of items coming into the office each month. Once Warhol had filled a box, it would be sealed, dated, and packed away; currently the Warhol Museum in Pittsburgh houses the 600+ boxes.)

The daily rhythms of the Factory were legendary. (In the mid-seventies, Factory lunches were procured from the gourmet Italian grocery store Balducci's, and usually picked up by Brigid Berlin, who became a Factory regular in the early sixties and a full-time Factory employee shortly after Warhol moved to 860 Broadway. A typical order would consist of curried chicken salad, string bean salad, pasta salad, sliced tomatoes and broccoli in oil, and several loaves of bread.)

It was also the site of one of art history's most famous first meetings. (The first time that Warhol and Basquiat met, after their early encounters on the street, was for a lunch date at 860 Broadway arranged by Warhol's ambitious Swiss dealer Bruno Bischofberger.)

Production here was industrial in scale. (While 860 Broadway played host to the planning and designing of all the new series, as well as to any background painting that was called for, the silkscreening itself—whether onto painted canvas or printmaker's paper—was outsourced to a contractor named Rupert Jasen Smith, who had replaced Alexander Heinrici in the last years of the '70s.)

Eventually the enterprise outgrew the space. (At first, Warhol had resented Fred Hughes's insistence that they needed a bigger headquarters than they had at 860 Broadway, so there'd be room for Interview and the video projects to continue expanding.) In time he relented, and (on October 19, 1981, he laid out $2 million, most of it borrowed, for the purchase and renovation of the ConEd building, as most seemed to call it. A few weeks later, he was raving about his new acquisition as "just sensational, so beautiful, you just can't believe it. . . The best thing is the roof terrace, it's like a terrace for a beautiful, glamorous apartment.")

The transition dragged on. (Warhol might have closed on the ConEd building in late '81, but it took until the spring of '83 for the big renovation to be close enough to completion for anyone to move in. The staff at Interview arrived first, and Warhol played his old trick of making them carry the contents of their desks to their new workplace sixteen blocks north. "My back was just killing me," Paige Powell recalled.) In the emptying rooms Warhol found a strange freedom, and (By the end of June 1984, the studio at 860 Broadway was left with Warhol and Brigid Berlin as its only occupants.) As one colleague recalled, ("He got to turn 860 Broadway into a big mess," Vincent Fremont recalled. "It became an artist's studio, in total.")

The scale of the operation being moved was staggering: (To get an idea of the scale of Warhol's enterprise, consider the transfer from 860 Broadway to the ConEd building, even after the Interview staff had already installed themselves there: A moving company estimated that it would take twenty men working to load four 40-foot trucks, over four Saturdays, to get everything transferred, at a cost of $35,000.) In the end, this address stood as (the fourth Factory location for Warhol, who had previously occupied smaller space at 231 East Forty-Seventh Street, 33 Union Square West, and 860 Broadway, but the first to be given a full architectural treatment.)

Later Lives

After Warhol, the building continued to host new tenants and new stories. Among them was a bar remembered simply as (Underground, located at) (The Underground: 860 Broadway at 17th Street).

In more recent decades the ground floor became a familiar retail fixture: (PETCO 860 Broadway (at Union Square W). With a selection of over 10,000 items, including food, toys, treats, and petcare products, this store is a pet owner's treasure trove. There are sections for cats, dogs, birds, fish, and even reptiles!)

The store even became a hub for animal rescue, home to volunteers who recalled: (My wife and I volunteer for KittyKind, an organization providing care for the cats who are up for adoption at Petco. We clean their cages and play with them. They call it "socializing them.'" I call it fun.)
