I. Origins in the Soil of Old Manhattan

Long before the elegant pile of (pink brick with light gray Belleville sandstone trim) rose above the (Flatiron District), the land upon which it sits belonged to the agrarian geography of colonial Manhattan. It formed part of one of those great manorial holdings—an entry in the block check list recording (903-902-875)—whose provenance the antiquarians of a later age would labor to reconstruct. This was (THE JAMES DUANE ESTATE (GRAMERCY SEAT)), an assemblage carefully parceled: (For the 4 A. tract (A)—849-848. For the 10 A. tract (B)—849-901-875-849. For the 6 A. tract (C)—878-904-902-901-878.) The manner of its acquisition was patrician and piecemeal, for (James Duane acquired this farm in three parcels. A. A parcel at the south-west corner, adjoining the Tiebout farm, 4 acres more or less, from Judith Stuyvesant. B. A parcel of 10 acres, more or less, from Gerardus Stuyvesant. C. A parcel containing 6 acres or thereabouts, from John Watts Jr.)

Abutting this holding lay the (CORNELIUS TIEBOUT FARM), styled (Roxborough) and catalogued under (Block Check List. 559). Its geometry defied precision, for (The Tiebout farm comprised four parcels which are here called A, B, C, and D, but which cannot be defined very accurately. Some other small pieces of land may have entered into the earliest farm. It is known that they were all part of the Stuyvesant farm.) The earliest occupants of these tracts bore the names of the Dutch and English yeomanry: (A. Land of Jan Pietersen Slot. B. Land of Hans Jacobsen van Bern. C. Land of Pieter Jansen Slot (formerly of Jan Thomassen). D. Land of John Clapp.)

By the early nineteenth century the neighborhood had begun its slow metamorphosis from farmland to fashionable precinct. The municipal registers offer only the barest trace of settlement: the Blue Book of 1815 inscribed upon the lot the single family name (Varian), attaching to it a bare reference to (0 Structures)—an empty parcel awaiting its destiny.

II. The Antebellum Streetscape

As the metropolis crept northward, the corner acquired buildings. The mid-century cartographers, meticulous in their fire-insurance surveys, catalogued a small cluster: by 1853 the site held (3 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings second class (slate or metal roof not coped) with stores under, Brick or Stone Store first class, Framed Store first class with skylights lighting the top floor only). A decade and a half later the record had grown sparser in detail but no less telling in density, the 1867 plan noting simply (3 structures; Building, Building, Building). This was a corner already committed to commerce, its (stores under) anticipating the retail grandeur to come. Nor was it wholly given over to trade in dishes and silver; the city directories preserve, for the adjacent number, the humble notice under (EMBROIDERIES AND DESIGNING) of (Shepherd, J. B., 891 Broadway.)

III. The Rise of the Ladies' Mile

The neighborhood's destiny as a merchant quarter was sealed in the years following the Civil War, when the great emporia of the age migrated uptown to cluster about Union Square. (Shops also moved on to Broadway, just north of Union Square. Lord & Taylor opened its new store at the southwest comer of Broadway and Twentieth Street in 1871.) The firm that would give this corner its name arrived amid a distinguished company, for (Other stores whose names are still familiar moved into the Union Square area in the following years: F. A. O. Schwarz at 42 East Fourteenth Street, Gorham & Company at the northwest comer of Broadway and Nineteenth Street, and W. & J. Sloane at the southeast comer of Broadway and Nineteenth Street.)

The vestiges of an older, aristocratic order still lingered nearby, for (The old Peter Goelet mansion, where peacocks once strutted in the yard, stood at the northeast comer of Broadway and Nineteenth Street until its demolition in 1898.) It is a felicitous irony of the record that the very family whose (peacocks once strutted in the yard) should be the patrons who erected the silver merchant's palace across the way. Here, in short, was one of the great (RETAIL BUSINESSES NEAR LADIES MILE: 1860s-1880s)—catalogued under the terse rubric (Name: Gorham Building, Date: 1883, Architect: Edward Kendall, Location: Broadway and Nineteenth Street (northwest corner)).

IV. The Gorham Building Conceived

The structure that has defined this address for a century and a half was raised in a single, confident campaign. (Was built in 1884.) In the taxonomy of the landmark rolls it appears as the (GORHAM MANUFACTURING COMPANY BUILDING), a work of (1883-84; alterations, 1893, 1912), situated squarely at (889-891 Broadway, Manhattan). Its authorship belonged to (Architects: Edward Hale Kendall; 1912 ALTERATION, JOHN H. DUNCAN), and it received the imprimatur of official protection when it was (Designated: June 19, 1984). Standing among the endangered oeuvre of its designer, the (Gorham Building is one of the few surviving buildings designed by Edward Hale Kendall.)

Its patronage was aristocratic and its purpose commercial. It was (Erected for Robert and Ogden Goelet when this section of Broadway was a fashionable shopping district, the building housed the store of the Gorham Manufacturing Company, a producer of fine silver, in its lower two floors, with apartments above.) It was, in the language of the guidebooks, raised (for its retail outlet m the exclusive ladies' Mile shopping district). More precisely, the design (placed six stories of bachelor apartments atop two stories of retail space leased by the silver manufacturer whose name identified the building.) In its hybrid program the building was strikingly ahead of its epoch, being (one of New York's earliest mixed-use Building), for (Its first two floors served as Gorham's retail store, but the rest was used as bachelor apartments)—a scheme the connoisseurs would restate as an arrangement in which (only the lower two floors were occupied by the store; the upper floors were rented as bachelor apartments.) That mingling of functions remains its signal distinction, for the building is (unusual for its early combination of commercial and residential use.)

V. Kendall's Queen Anne Vision

Kendall clothed his commission in the picturesque idiom then fashionable, producing what would later be judged (An unusual remaining example of the Queen Anne style)—a work (noted for its picturesque massing and rich ornamental detail.) It was, in a more effusive appraisal, (This Queen Anne structure ... one of New York's earliest mixed-use Building), and, in the fullest praise, (the deliberately quaint and picturesque Queen Anne style in its full expression.) The materials were chosen for their chromatic richness: (The structure is constructed of pink brick with light gray Belleville sandstone trim; the high-pitched slate roof with green copper elements adds to the building's appeal.) Its decorative program grew richer as the eye ascended, for (The ornament, which includes decorated segmental arches and panels embellished with sunflowers and floral motifs, is more elaborate at the top.)

VI. The Verdict of the Critics

The building arrived precisely at the moment when the tide of taste was turning against the manner it embodied. (By the time the Gorham Building was finished, the taste for the Queen Anne was waning, as could be seen in Mariana Van Rensselaer's reluctance to 'really approve' of the design, 'with its trivial "Queen Anne" detail appropriate only to a structure one-tenth its size.') The trade press was sharper still.
