Ink, Terra Cotta, and Time: The Layered Life of 33 East 17th Street

Long before any monument of brick and terra cotta laid claim to this parcel, the ledgers testify to a landscape of near-emptiness. In the primordial record of the neighborhood, (In the 1815 Blue Book, on 33 East 17th Street, there is reference to 0 structures.) Within a single generation the ground had gathered substance and enterprise, for (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 33 East 17th Street, there is reference to 4 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Brick or Stone Extension, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Brick or Stone Extension, Brick or Stone Store first class, Brick or Stone Store first class.) By the years that followed, the block took its character from a celebrated neighbor whose name became its landmark, for (In the 1867 Dripps Plan, on 33 East 17th Street, there is written 'Everett House' as well as reference to 5 Structures; Building, Building, Building, Building, Building.)

Then arose the structure destined to bestow upon the address its lasting identity. The essential fact is stated with disarming brevity: it (Was built in 1881.) One authoritative account traces the circumstances of its conception, recording that (William Schickel's Century Company Building (1880-81), facing Union Square at 33 East Seventeenth Street, between Broadway and Fourth Avenue, developed as a speculative venture by the dry-goods company Arnold Constable, was principally leased by the company that gave the building its name, the publishers of the popular Century Magazine as well as St. Nicholas Magazine, which was written for children.) Behind the enterprise stood a formidable dynasty of merchant-landlords, for (The Century Building was commissioned as a speculative undertaking by the Arnold and Constable families. In addition to their department store, they were among the largest landholders in late nineteenth-century New York. For much of the Century Building's life, it was the home of the celebrated Century Magazine.)

The architecture itself constitutes the building's most eloquent testimony, and observers have lavished it with description. One rendering captures its inviting warmth: (Warm red brick, crisp bluestone trim, and a two-story oriel window give this Queen Anne style commercial building a welcoming domestic aspect. Richly detailed terra-cotta panels and crisply carved stone surround the ground floor entrance, embellish the facade, and reappear in the treatment of the top floor dormers.) A guidebook renders it with tender affection, calling it a place where (This red brick and whitestone charmer is where the popular Century magazine (for grown-ups) and St. Nicholas (for boys and girls) were published before the century turned.) Yet not every eye was charmed; one critic, surveying it from the vantage of Union Square, delivered a more skeptical verdict: (The old Century Building, headquarters of the long-gone Century Illustrated Magazine, is across the street on the south corner of 17th Street facing Union Square. It was built in 1881 to the designs of William Schickel, and while the Queen Anne pile might have worked somewhere else, it seems oddly uncomfortable here. Union Square can accommodate flamboyant show-offs and it can accommodate derelicts, but not genteel matrons.)

Its scarcity among surviving specimens of its kind has drawn repeated emphasis. One scholar situates it within the wider architectural canon: (The Century Building, former headquarters of one of the most popular magazines of the nineteenth century, stands at 33 East 17th Street between Park Avenue South and Broadway. Built in 1881 and designed by William Schickel, this red-brick-and-limestone Queen Anne pile comes straight out of Victorian London. It is one of the few extant major Queen Anne buildings in Manhattan.) Another, tracing the perambulation around the square, attends to its commanding presence upon the streetscape, noting that (Directly across the street is the former Century Building, 33 East 17th Street, now a Barnes & Noble superstore (William Schickel, 1880). The red brick. Queen Anne-style structure is one of the finest examples of the style and was built to house the many publishing activities of the Century Company. Among its imprints were the Century and St. Nicholas magazines and a host of fine books. It joined with D. Appleton & Company in 1933 and. after another merger, became Appleton-Century-Crofts.)

The building's literary lineage was uncommonly distinguished. As one account elaborates, (The Century Building is one of the few surviving commercial examples of the Queen Anne style in the city, distinguished by its picturesque mixture of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English motifs. The tall oriel window, gambrel roof, prominent chimneys, and molded terra cotta ornament are all characteristic of the style. Although classical details are commonly used (such as the Jacobean doorway), these are combined in an unusual and thoroughly nonclassical way. The building takes its name from the Century Company, publishers of The Century Magazine and St. Nicholas Magazine, which had offices there. The Century was a noted literary publication, carrying work by Mark Twain, Henry James, Walt Whitman, and Herman Melville. Another prominent tenant of the building was the architect George B. Post.) Its roster of imprints and authors was equally storied, for (This seven-story Barnes & Noble was once the home of the Century Company, publishers of the once popular Century and St. Nicholas magazines and books by Edith Wharton, Mark Twain, and Rudyard Kipling, 1881, William Schickel.)

The very fabric of the façade may be parsed like a meticulous architectural catalogue: its idiom is declared (Queen-Anne, neo-Grec: Secondary Façade 18th Street,) its base rendered in (Crystal - 213 213 213,) its façade cast in (Brick Red - 196 47 69,) wrought at the base from (Red Brick, Wyoming Bluestone) and across the elevation from (Brick, Terra Cotta, Wyoming Bluestone, Iron.) The entrance is composed of (3 Bays, Pedimented, Arched, Fanlight, Cast Iron Ornament, Tuscan Pilasters, Console Bracket Capital,) the fenestration articulated by (Segmental Arch Lintel (3-5), Stone Keystone, 2-story Oriel, Window Panes: [1/1] (w/ Transom),) the masonry enlivened with (Running Bond, Rusticated Brick Pilasters, Garlands, Shells, Grotesques, Dentil Course, Egg & Dart Course, Swags,) and the whole crowned by a roofline of (Gambrel Roof, Cast Iron Cornice, 2-Story Dormers, Balustrade, Dentils, Frieze, Skew Corbels, Sunflower Terra Cotta Finials.)

The cartographic record chronicles the building's steady maturation across the generations. As the century drew to its close, (In the 1897 Bromleys Atlas, on 33 East 17th Street, there is reference to 1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building.) A dozen years prior, a resident enterprise had already inscribed itself upon the surveyors' plates, for (In the 1885 Robinsons Atlas, on 33 East 17th Street, there is written 'John Son & Eanlloner Upholstery Goods' as well as reference to 1 Structure; Brick building.) As the new century advanced, the loft designation came to prevail: (In the 1916 Atlas of the Borough of Manhattan, on 33 East 17th Street, there is written 'Lofts' as well as reference to 1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building with Store and Elevators.) That same designation endured a decade and a half onward, for (In the 1930 Land Book of the Borough of Manhattan, on 33 East 17th Street, there is written 'Lofts' as well as reference to 1 Structure; 6 Story Brick building with Store and Elevators.) And a full quarter century later the character remained unchanged: (In the 1955 Manhattan Land Book, on 33 East 17th Street, there is written 'Lofts' as well as reference to 1 Structure; 6 Story Brick or Stone or Concrete building with Store and Elevators.)

The succession of tenants who traversed these lofts and chambers composes a vivid cross-section of professional Manhattan. Among the architects who took quarters here we encounter (HUBERT & PIRSSON Philip G. Hubert. James W. Pirsson Est'd. 5 years. (1843-1916) AL 1886-AAA 1895 33 E. 17th, Century Bldg.); and (MAYNICKE, ROBERT (1849-1913) Architect, Inspector of Schools, Twenty-eighth District (1896-9)), whose ledger records (1890-4 33 E. 17th) amid his procession of addresses. Others pursued the ornamental arts, and beneath the rubric of artistic decorators appears the (Lafarge Decorative Art Co., 33 E. 17th.) The building's mercantile vitality was memorialized in a striking interior photograph, catalogued as (128. Office of Earl and Wilson Collar Manufacturers, 33 East 17th Street (1903).) That scene, more fully drawn, reveals an emblem of the era's corporate order: (A business that rode the crest of fashion, Earl and Wilson was one of a number of makers of men's detachable collars, which were of paper, celluloid or starched cloth. The labyrinth of banistered corrals provided well-defined spaces for executives and employees, and it symbolized a well ordered bureaucracy. The pilastered screen with leaded colored-glass windows further departmentalized the workers. Electricity has been brought down to the desk lamps. No females are visible in this headquarters of male attire, which enjoyed a lucrative reign until the whim of fashion changed.)

The edifice's enshrinement as a landmark arrived amid a broader groundswell of solicitude for the future of Union Square, when (Concerns about the fate of historic buildings on other sites around the square led to the designation as landmarks of the Lincoln Building (R. H. Robertson, 1890), 1-3 Union Square West; the Bank of the Metropolis (Bruce Price, 1903), 31 Union Square West; the Century Building (William Schickel, 1881), 33 East Seventeenth Street; the Everett Building (Starrett & Van Vleck, 1908), 45 East Seventeenth Street; and the Germania Life Insurance Company Building (D'Oench &. Yost, 1911), later the Guardian Life Building, 50 Union Square East.) The official record affirms its stature and its rebirth, describing the (Century Building, now Barnes & Noble Bookstore 33 East 17th Street (William Schickel, 1880-81). Designated 1986. The red brick Century Building is a rare New York example of a Queen Anne- style commercial structure. Erected to house the prestigious Century Publishing Company, publisher of the Century and St, Nicholas magazines, the building is characteristic of the finest Queen Anne design in its free use of classical motifs in stone and terracotta and its employment of the style's trademark sunflowers. After standing vacant for many years, the braiding was converted into a bookstore, and the facade received an award-winning restoration.)

The chapter of its literary revival, following that painstaking restoration, is amply documented. In this metamorphosis, (In 1995 Barnes &. Noble transformed the 100,000-square-foot Queen Anne-style Century Building (Wilbam Schickel, 1880-81) once home to a famous literary magazine, 33 East Seventeenth Street, facing Union Square. Judith Saltzman of Li-Saltzman restored the facade, and fames Cohen was charged with maintaining die character of the landmark interiors, which were listed on the National Register, while SBLM Architects. By 3 Group designed the bookstore itself in keeping with the bookseller's overall design standards. Taking advantage of the high ceilings and such architectural features as Corinthian capped cast-iron columns and consider able original woodwork, the effect was impressive, even though it lacked the intimacy and charm of many of the traditional bookstores such as Shakespeare Company and Endicott that the superstores were rapidly driving out of business.) Thereafter it takes its place, unassumingly, among the city's cultural landmarks as (Barnes and Noble, Union Square: 33 East 17th Street near Broadway, Flatiron.)

In its second life the store flourished as a nexus of sociability and letters alike. Contemporaries observed that (Barnes & Noble superstores, with their loungy and brainy atmosphere and two-person reading tables, are a magnet for those on the prowl and their rightful prey. In late mornings and early evenings on the weekends, the branch at Union Square (33 East 17th Street, 253-0810) comes alive with singles who judge each other by what they're reading. For my money. Travel and Cookbooks are the two best sections.) It became a venue for public readings, catalogued at (UNION SQUARE, 33 East 17th Street (between Park Avenue and Broadway); (212) 253-0810; Saturday at 2:00 P.M.) Among the luminaries who graced its podium was (MARGARET ATWOOD With more then 25 books under her belt, she reads from her most recent novel, the blind Assassin. Barnes & Noble. 33 E. 17th, 253 -0810, at 7:30.) And upon another evening the stage belonged to (LAURA ESQUIVEL A reading in Spanish of her new novel Swift As Desire/Tan Veloz Como el Deseo Her editor translates in English Barnes & Noble. 33 E. 17th, 253-0810, at 7.30.)

And so the address, once no more than four modest dwellings and stores standing in the shadow of the Everett House, ascended to become one of the most cherished surviving Queen Anne commercial buildings in Manhattan—home first to a celebrated house of letters, and later to a thronged bookstore keeping vigil over Union Square.
