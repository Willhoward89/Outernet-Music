I. Tabula Rasa: The Empty Corner and the Westmoreland Years

Before any monument to political power rose upon this corner, the land lay empty. The earliest cartographic record notes that in the 1815 Blue Book, on 44 Union Square East, there is reference to 0 structures. Within a few decades, however, the parcel had been developed in the refined idiom of an increasingly fashionable district: by mid-century, the 1853 Perris Maps record 4 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with Framed Extension and skylights lighting the top floor only, repeated across the lot and terminating in a final dwelling with Framed Extension. The neighborhood context is essential here, for Union Square was originally called Union Place in 1811, and During the late nineteenth century, 14th Street was the midway point for Ladies' Mile, a walkway of swanky shops that extended from Broadway and 8th to 23rd Street. This commercial golden age proved transient, for By the turn of the century, businesses and art galleries traveled uptown to Madison Square.

By the era of the 1867 Dripps Plan, the record still shows 4 structures; Building, Building, Building, Building. This is precisely the moment of great consequence for the history of the site, for the political machine that would one day define it moved into a building on this site in 1867. Indeed, this very parcel is identified as the site of what was the second location of the great political machine Tammany Hall, the whole being just east of the Con Edison building. The subsequent decades saw the parcel transformed into a hostelry: the 1885 Robinsons Atlas records the name West Moreland Hotel alongside 2 Structures; Brick building, Brick building, and by the 1897 Bromleys Atlas the West Moreland Hotel had expanded into 3 Structures; 5 Story Brick building, 7 Story Brick building, 3 Story Brick building with Basement. The 1916 Atlas preserves the name in its contracted form, Westmoreland, describing 3 Structures; 5 Story Brick building with Store, 7 Story Brick building with Store and Elevator, 3 Story Brick building with Basement and Store. We know, too, that a nearby establishment had faltered: The Clarendon Hotel, at the south-east corner of 17th St. and Fourth Ave., has been closed.—N. Y. Times, O 14, 1898. Nor was the immediate vicinity bereft of literary distinction, for the last home of Jeannette L. Gilder, long editor of the Critic and arresting, if sometimes amusing, figure in the literary life of the town a generation ago, was at 100 East Seventeenth Street—that same Journalist; born Flushing, New York, October 3rd, 1849.

II. Born of the Sons of Liberty: The Society and Its Ascendancy

To understand the building, one must understand the institution that commissioned it. The New York Tammany Society was founded about 1786, and organized under a constitution in 1789 as a charitable and patriotic order. Alternatively dated, the organization is described as a club... founded in 1788 as an outgrowth of the patriotic Sons of Liberty and called The Society of St. Tammany or Columbian Order, itself An outgrowth of the eighteenth-century Tammany Society. From these origins It has gradually developed into one of the most powerful political influences in the history of our nation. Its very name carried an invented genealogy, Deriving its name from a sachem of the Delaware Indians (Tammany—one of the chiefs of the Algonquins, living on the banks of the Delaware River, who for years held undisputed sway over Eastern America).

In its earliest incarnation the society was a genuinely reformist body, promoting public education, universal suffrage for white males, and helping to abolish debtors' prisons; as well as providing support for newly arrived Irish, French, and German immigrants; it quickly became affiliated with the newly formed Democratic Party. But power corroded purpose. By the mid-nineteenth century, Tammany was firmly in charge of the municipal government, and under the leadership of the now infamous "Boss" William Tweed. Indeed the Tammany Society, New York's Democratic political machine, virtually controlled the city during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Thus did this great society, from its very organization became a dominating factor in the councils of the Democratic Party and has commanded such a power in local, state, and even national politics, that the name ''Tammany" has become known world over. So complete was its grip that it is observed New York politics arc so intimately identified with Tammany Hall it is said that the Democratic Party in New York City is so completely controlled by this powerful organization that no nominee has even a fighting chance for victory unless endorsed by Tammany, while In State politics, also. Tammany commands an influence which is wide spread and generally conceded to be a power that must be thoroughly recognized.

III. Shrine or Satan's Headquarters: A Portrait Drawn in Contradictions

Few institutions in American civic life have provoked so bifurcated a reputation. One contemporary account opens with a near-incantatory declaration: TAMMANY HALL!!—revered by its constituents; feared by neutrals; hated by its opponents! To its faithful, it was sacred ground: Tammany Hall is the shrine at which hundreds of thousands of loyal democrats worship, and point with pride to its achievements. To its detractors, it was a den of iniquity: On the other hand, its opponents damn Tammany Hall as the hellish headquarters for graft, corruption, and misused power! The polarity invited the rhetorical marvel: Has any institution ever organized outside of fanatical religious circles, been so beloved or so hated; so lauded or so execrated; so eulogized or so condemned! The scandal was not merely historical but immediate, for the chronicler recorded that a new legislative investigation of New York City's administration has Tammany under fire on charges of various misdeeds, though he conceded it would be premature to forecast the outcome.

The defense rested upon demonstrated generosity—noting that Tammany's history reveals the names of many of America's most prominent and respected sons, who are or have been enrolled as members, and that Records also show that vast sums have been donated to charity or expended in relieving distress—not only in New York and America, but all over the world, such as generous contributions to Japan's earthquake sufferers, the Cuban Freedom Fund, the Irish Bond Issue, the Messina earthquake disaster, etc. The prosecution, by contrast, saw only transaction—those who can see no good in Tammany insisting that Tammany is an agency of Satan and that policy prompts these benefactions, that all this is merely a part for a price paid for votes and popularity. The fair-minded observer was therefore counseled toward caution, noting that any estimate of Tammany to be formed by a stranger, must be based largely on whether information or data concerning the institution is furnished by a Tammany supporter, or a Tammany opponent. A later chronicler of the criminal classes leaves no such ambiguity: Tammany Hall was a notoriously corrupt political organization with very close ties to the criminal underworld of the Lower East Side, a byword for municipal corruption and the Democratic political machine that held the city in a corrupt stranglehold for almost two centuries.

IV. Federal Hall Reborn: The Commission and the Architecture

The building we now consider was conceived at a moment of dynastic reinvention—His fall, and the emergence of a "New Tammany" in the 1920s, refashioned as a "good government" organization, has been identified by some as the impetus for the construction of this New Tammany Hall. Late in 1927, the Society of Tammany sold its headquarters on East 14th Street and announced the construction of a new Tammany Hall. Plans for the new building were made public in January 1928. Its design fell to the firms recorded as Thompson, Holmes & Converse and Charles B. Meyers—or, as elsewhere styled, THOMPSON, HOLMES & CONVERSE WITH CHARLES BRADFORD MEYERS. The intended result was to be a Colonial Revival structure built of red Harvard brick with granite trim (limestone was later substituted). Contemporary appraisal was favorable; the design was described as a dignified architectural treatment, one of the chief motifs of which arc the severe Colonial columns in the centers of the Union Square and the Seventeenth-street facades which recall the days of early American architecture.

The stylistic choice was no accident but a deliberate act of self-fashioning. The Colonial Revival was undoubtedly chosen not only because it was reminiscent of the architecture of the early republic, bur also because it symbolized the government established during that period. At its peak of power, in other words, the society erected this clubhouse as its headquarters. Most pointedly, the Union Square facade bears a striking resemblance to the original Federal Hall, where George Washington took the oath of office as the first President of the United States. The ideological motive is laid bare: By choosing the Colonial Revival style, with all that it symbolized, Tammany may have been attempting to create the image of a truly democratic force to counter the accusations that it was a corrupt political machine. It was, by the measure of a later survey, the trim neo-Federal-style brick-and-limestone building—and in a third account, a new marble building at 100 East 17th Street, comer of Fourth Avenue, the organization whose "Wigwam" had stood for decades in 14th Street and whose Democratic leaders still ruled New York.

A later and more technically precise account enriches the picture, characterizing This distinguished neo-Georgian facade, with its rusticated stone base, double-height pilasters, and sculptural reliefs in terra cotta and limestone, was inspired by the original Federal Hall (demolished 1812) on Wall Street, where the first President of the United States, George Washington, took his oath of office. The craftsmanship is described in granular detail: Clad with specially molded and oversized brick laid in Flemish bond, other neo-Georgian elements of the facade, such as the blind arches that frame the multilight sash windows, the fanlight transoms, wrought iron balconies, and semicircular attic window, deliberately evoke the dignity of early American architecture. Another surveyor concurs that The Union Square facade of the building including the first-floor balcony under the portico was deliberately modelled on that of New York's original Federal Hall on Wall Street (demolished 1812) where Washington was inaugurated as our nation's first President.

Among the façade's most eloquent survivals is a single emblem, noted with fine topographical precision: another liberty cap—across the street in the pediment of the old Tammany meeting place at 100 East Seventeenth Street—is eminently visible as it oversees the park and the Charles F. There especially during the 1930s, people came to speak their minds without fear of assault or arrest, amid friezes bearing idealized, almost life-size figures of Indians, pioneer families, horses, and buffaloes, portraying the American struggle for independence. Union Square had by then become a crucible of dissent—In the years prior to World War I, Union Square evolved into a forum for anarchists, socialists, Wobblies, and Communists, where they took to soapboxes to espouse their political leanings—its large assemblies oftentimes grew into dashes with the authorities, the most notorious being a Depression-era workers protest of 35,000 that led to public outcry after police attacked one hundred of the protestors, such that the square became the soul of militant political movements in the City.

VI. Zenith and Downfall: The Reckoning of the Machine

The new hall was raised at the very apex of the society's fortunes. Tammany Hall reached its zenith in 1928. The triumvirate of leadership was formidable: Smith was a powerful and widely respected governor; Walker was an extraordinarily popular mayor, and George W. Olvany, a college graduate, was the boss. The physical monument followed—A new building was completed in 1929 in Union Square at 17th Street—during which interval even refromers had few Criticisms of Tammany Hall.

Yet the completion of the edifice coincided, with grim irony, with the reversal of the society's fortunes: In 1929, when the building was completed, Tammany had again fallen out of favor. But the organizations fortunes soon changed. The reckoning arrived through the courts: Investigations of civic corruption by Samuel Seabury led to Walker's resignation in 1932, an event elsewhere recorded as By the early 1930s the extent of the society's graft and corruption was widely recognized, and Tammany Mayor James J. Walker was forced to resign in 1932. Political miscalculation compounded disgrace: John F. Curry, Olvany's successor, sought to block Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidential nomination, allowing Flynn, no longer an ally of Tammany Hall, to become the strongest link between the Democratic White House and the city. The electoral deathblow came at the polls: In 1933 Mayor John P. O'Brien, the incumbent and a loyalist chosen by Curry, finished last in a three-way mayoral election won by Fiorello H. La Guardia, who led a coalition opposed to Tammany Hall that remained in place for 12 years. By mid-century the organization was spent: By the time the Democrats recaptured the mayoralty in 1945, Tammany Hall had virtually ceased to exist, although politicians bred in the organization continued to flourish into the 1950s and beyond.

VII. From Union Hall to Footlights: The Long Afterlife of a Monument

Financial ruin forced the surrender of the building itself. Unable to meet mortgage payments, the sachems sold their building to Local 91 of the International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union in 1943, after which it became a major center for union activity. A parallel account confirms that by 1943, the Society sold the building to Local 91 of the International Ladies Garment Workers Union, which occupied it until 1984, while a third concurs A decade later the society sold its meeting hall to the International Ladies Garment Workers Union. In its union years the structure housed the International Ladies Garment Workers Union Local 99-105, and its 499-seat auditorium has been used by the Roundabout Theater Company, a group with 20,000 subscribers.

Before a new chapter in the performing arts began—That year, the Local entered into a lease agreement with the Roundabout Theatre, which renovated and occupied the building's auditorium until 1991, when it moved to 45th Street. The theatrical pedigree of the auditorium is considerable. In 1984 the Roundabout converted a 1,000-seat auditorium in the old Tammany Hall into an intimate 500-seat theatre, seating 499 (Orchestra 358; Mezzanine, 141), and under its Artistic Director Gene Feist—who, Since its founding in 1965, has built the Roundabout into one of the major nonprofit theatrical institutions in the country—A number of well-known actors got their New York start here, including Amanda Plummer, David Dukes, B. D. Wong, Kate Burton, Danny Glover, and Jerry Zaks. In the interval between tenancies, Local 91 subsequently leased the auditorium to producer Raymond L. Gaspard, and then to the owners of Minetta Lane Theatre. The building's institutional diversification continued: In 1994, the Local began leasing portions of the building not occupied by the theater to the New York Film Academy; in 2001, the entire building was sold to Liberty Theatres and four years later the air rights were conveyed to US Development LLC.

The former union meeting hall acquired a new identity: In 2001, they, in turn, sold it to Liberty Theaters, who remodeled the union's former meeting hall—once called the Roosevelt Auditorium—into the present Union Square Theater. This reborn venue, a large Off Broadway house, has played host to such shows as Wit, The Laramie Project, BatBoy, and others. Among its notable engagements was a 1995 production in which Vanessa Redgrave and Eileen Atkins star in, and Zoe Caldwell directs, this play based on the letters of Sackville West and Woolf, as well as a benefit at which Vanessa Redgrave and Eileen Atkins host an evening of words and music to benefit the children of Sarajevo, joined by Glenn Close, Stockard Channing, Mira Furlan, John Turturro, Christopher Reeve, Harvey Keitel, Simon Shaheen, Joseph Brodsky, Paul Auster, Ariel Dorfman, Susan Sontag, Tony Kushner, and Wendy Wasserstein. The venue carries deeper echoes still, for it was here that Bly delivered a series of lectures here about her record-breaking trip around the globe, though in time Bly's editors, once overshadowed, stopped promoting her, and she left The World, feeling she was underpaid and underappreciated. It acquired, too, a measure of folklore, being reportedly haunted by a phantom that lived in a crawl space and had eyes like a cal that glowed in the dark, a spectral tenant whose mischief—Windows and a chandelier once started making a racket on their own—ceased only when the maintenance man demanded that whoever - or whatever - knock it off so he could get some sleep. And the sounds stopped.

Meanwhile the film school took firm root, advertising itself under the banner LEARN FILM MAKING, with SUMMER WORKSHOPS AT PRINCETON UNIVERSITY & NEW YORK FILM ACADEMY, promising that students would DIRECT YOUR OWN SHORT FILMS IN OUR HANDS-ON INTENSIVE WORKSHOPS USING 16MM ARRIFLEX CAMERAS. LEARN DIRECTING, STORY, CAMERA, SOUND AND EDITING IN A TOTAL IMMERSION COURSE DESIGNED AND TAUGHT BY AWARD WINNING INSTRUCTORS.—all under the motto "CARPE DIEM", at a TUITION $4000—noting that the academy was FORMERLY LOCATED AT TRIBECA FILM CENTER. The building, in short, became a palimpsest of civic lives: NEW YORK FILM ACADEMY, FORMERLY ROUNDABOUT THEATRE; LOCAL 91 OF ILGWU; TAMMANY HALL.

VIII. The Sole Physical Remnant: A Monument to Memory

As the twentieth century closed, the building's significance as an artifact of civic history came under formal consideration. One preservationist judged it such a great example of Colonial Revival, of a time when a group tried to burnish its crooked reputation by linking itself to early American ideals, insisting that the history of Tammany Hall, shabby as it is, is also a big part of our past, and this building is the sole physical remnant of that. Officialdom concurred, recognizing it as the only surviving headquarters building of Tammany and now a byword for municipal corruption. With characteristic New York wit, there was even a proposal that the Tammany Hall building be converted into a "Museum of Political Corruption"; however, one wag observed that "it just wouldn't be large enough!" The landmark was duly recognized on its date of designation, October 29, 2013. Through every mutation of its use—even as The building is now being converted to office and retail use—one promise has been kept: the exterior, including the Liberty Cap on the pediment facing Union Square, long a symbol of the society, has been preserved.
