THE PALIMPSEST OF A METROPOLITAN CORNER

I. THE LEASEHOLD GENESIS AND THE ALCHEMY OF LAND VALUE

The genealogy of this parcel commences not in the clamor of commerce but in the quietude of ecclesiastical devotion—and, antecedent to both, in the humble instrument of the ground lease. Within the mnemonic reservoir of an octogenarian's recollections, we discover that (Ex-Mayor Daniel F. Tiemann's father and uncle leased twenty-one lots on Twenty-third Street near Broadway at $3.00 per annum. They had previously leased on Fifth Avenue, Nineteenth and Twentieth streets, sixty lots at $50.00 per year.) These were speculative holdings in a precinct still in the throes of self-definition, and their pecuniary appreciation would prove nothing short of meteoric, for (In 1840 a portion of the lots leased by them, 1820, on Fifth Avenue between Eighteenth and Nineteenth streets, was sold for $27,000, and Arnold & Constable in 1868 paid $375,000 for them.)

II. THE PEREGRINATION OF A CONGREGATION

Yet antecedent to the arrival of the mercantile aristocracy, it was the congregants who consecrated this ground. The sanctuary destined to lend the corner its earliest civic identity had itself undertaken a protracted northward peregrination through the burgeoning metropolis. As the antiquarian topographer meticulously recorded, (Then called | "Duane St. Church." Congr. removed to S. E. cor. Fifth Ave. and 19th St. 1852; thenceforward known as Fifth Avenue Presby. Ch. (q. v.)—King's Handbook, 366.) The impetus behind this migration recapitulated the very forces that had earlier dislodged the flock from Cedar Street, for, as the chronicler of the parish observed, (The same reasons that made it necessary to remove from Cedar street, operated with greater urgency to demand another change of locality.) The centrifugal drift of its membership toward the fashionable uptown wards proved decisive, since (Some of the members of the church, who had removed to the upper part of the city, had commenced efforts to build a church for their a accommodation and for others who were disposed to join them.) The scheme crystallized amid a season of collective resolve, whereupon (In the fall of 1850 a proposition w a s made to the Duane' street church to sell their edifice, and to unite in the enterprise, and give a call to their late pastor. This plan was adopted with great unanimity, the call was made and accepted, and measures were immediately taken to procure a site and erect a church.)

III. THE CONSECRATION, THE DIVINE, AND THE DIASPORA

The ceremonies of installation and dedication ensued in due and solemn course. As the parochial historian faithfully attests, (On the 12th of November, 1851, Rev. James W. Alexander, D.D., was installed pastor of the Duane street church by the Presbytery of New York. The congregation met for public worship in the chapel of the University until the dedication of the new edifice on the corner of Fifth Avenue and Nineteenth street, which took place December 19, 1852, and the corporate name was changed.) The fiscal footing of this new house of worship was, from its very inception, one of enviable solvency, inasmuch as (the building was erected and occupied for the first time in December, 1852, and the pews were sold, realizing a sum sufficient to pay all the cost.) Grief, too, inscribed itself upon the annals, for (Rev. Dr. Alexander was removed by death on the 31st of July, 1859, the State of Virginia, whither he had gone for the benefit of his health.)

The architectural provenance and subsequent vicissitudes of this edifice were singularly extraordinary. As the discerning historian of the avenue elucidates, (The Filth Avenue Presbyterian Church, which counted many Madison Square residents among its congregation, was built on the southeast corner of Fifth Avenue and Nineteenth Street in 1852-53: the architect was Leopold Eidlitz, who designed other religious structures in the city. The subsequent history of the church was unusual: in 1875 it was sold at auction and presented by die buyer to the Central Presbyterian Church, which dismantled the building and re-erected it again on West Fifty-seventh Street.) A complementary survey of the thoroughfare corroborates the precise moment of exodus, recording that (Fifth Avenue Presbyterian, which had been located at the southeast corner of Fifth Avenue and Nineteenth Street since 1852, moved to the building it still occupies, at the northwest corner of Fifth Avenue and Fifty-fifth Street, in 1875.) A solitary lithographic caption enshrines the date above the image of its successor: (Image Caption: FIFTH AVENUE CHURCH erected 1875.)

IV. A GAZETTEER OF THE GENTEEL

The very register that memorialized the sanctuary likewise preserved a comprehensive inventory of the patrician households that flanked the avenue—a veritable social cartography of the era's beau monde: (1—Miss Frances A. Graham's school for girls (she succeeded Miss Lucy M. Green); 37 —Grosvenor House, northeast corner of 10th Street; 39—George N. Miller; 41—Mrs. David S. Kennedy; 47 Mrs. Lead Hawley; 49 William W. Parkin ; 51—Eli White; 53 James Lenox; 55—John Fishbee Sheafe; 57--Dr. James Lenox Banks; 81 John Brooks; Daniel Parish, southeast corner of 16th Street; 87—Dr. Robert G. Remsen; 99—Robert Lenox Kennedy.) Within the selfsame compendium, the sanctuary was noted with laconic precision amid the brownstone establishments: (Fifth Avenue Church (Dr. John Hall), southeast corner of 19th Street, afterwards moved to 55th Street and Fifth Avenue.)

V. THE DISTRICT RENDERED IN CARTOGRAPHIC INK

The mid-century surveys apprehended this precinct in the very throes of metamorphosis, its fabric yet entangled with domestic dwellings and modest shopfronts. Upon the Eighteenth Street parcel, the cartographers registered the coexistence of two houses of worship amid a heterogeneous congeries of construction: (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 9-13 East 18th Street, there is written 'Episcopal Church, Presbyterian church' as well as reference to 11 Structures; Framed dwellings, Framed Store first class, Brick or Stone Store second class...) Along the Broadway frontage, the parcels presented an equally variegated assemblage: (In the 1853 Perris Maps, on 881-887 Broadway, there is reference to 4 structures; Brick or Stone dwellings fourth class (shingle roof) with stores under, Brick or Stone dwellings first class (slate or metal roof & coped) with stores under, Framed dwellings with stores under and Framed Extension, Framed dwellings with stores under and Framed Extension.) By the epoch of the Dripps Plan, the density of habitation had grown positively prodigious along the block: (In the 1867 Dripps Plan, on 881-887 Broadway, there is reference to 14 structures; Building, Building, Building...), while the Eighteenth Street lot comprised (3 structures; Building, Building, Building.)

VI. THE APOTHEOSIS OF LADIES' MILE

Thereafter descended the merchant princes, and with them the wholesale transfiguration of the district. The architectural essayist situated the emporium within the grand dialectic of urban succession, discerning that (Neighborhoods change. What was originally a residential area becomes yesterday's first-class shopping center, today's commercial district, and maybe the site of tomorrow's renaissance. By the 1870s this area was entering the second stage of that urban progression.) He proceeded to elaborate: (Moving into it were New York's largest stores, stores that had started as small shops in lower Manhattan. By 1000 this uptown migration would be complete. with New York's finest stores lining Broadway solidly from Eighth to Twenty-third streets, and Sixth Avenue from Fourteenth to Twenty-third streets. Since this was the cream of New York's shopping, and since women were reputedly the principal patrons, the stretches became known as Ladies' Mile.)

The competitive stratagems of the period were rendered with vivid particularity, for (Competing retailers, however, chose to leapfrog the "Iron Palace" at its 9th Street location and follow the growth of the city north, clustering themselves farther up Broadway.) The animating cause resided in the pedigree of the avenue's inhabitants, inasmuch as (Elegant and powerful Fifth Avenue set the rules for the neighborhood's retail establishments. Lined with the townhouses of the rich and famous, its wealthy residents demanded shopping options close by. Soon Broadway between Union and Madison Squares offered a fine variety of dry goods stores, and developed into a prim and proper "carriage trade," whose customers always (at least in theory) rolled up in style.) Within the selfsame disquisition, the neighboring purveyors of gemstones executed their own translocation, for (Tiffany & Co. moved its fine emporium, called the "palace of jewels" — you get the point — to its new headquarters at Union Square West and 15th Street in 1870. They even put the French crown jewels on display here in 1887.)

VII. WHEN MAMMON SUPPLANTED THE SACRED

The ascendancy of the mercantile district was purchased at the expense of venerable ecclesiastical institutions, for, as the theorist of commercial form proclaimed, (American commercial architecture adopted a functional form: beauty was found in perfect mechanical efficiency.) As that same historian meticulously chronicled, (The real inroads of commercialization above Fourteenth Street were felt, however, when Renwick's Church of the Puritans was torn down in 1869 and replaced by yet another Kellum-designed cast-iron building, the five-story Tiffany Building, at the southwest comer of Fifteenth Street and Union Square.) It was into precisely this milieu that the great dry-goods palace arose. As the record attests, (Arnold Constable and Company opened a new store in 1869 in a marble-fronted five-story building (881 -883 Broadway) on the west side of Broadway between Eighteenth and Nineteenth streets, designed by Griffith Thomas (fig. 117). In 1873 Thomas added a huge two-story mansard roof for extra floor space and in 1877 a cast-iron replica of the Broadway facade was designed, again by Thomas, for the new Fifth Avenue extension.) In the very annum that Arnold Constable and Company broke ground, a rival house followed suit, for (Lord and Taylor moved into a highly ornate five-story castiron building, designed by James Giles, on the southwest corner of Broadway and Twentieth Street at 901 Broadway.)

VIII. THE EDIFICE AARON ARNOLD RAISED

The lineage of the firm extends into an even remoter antiquity, as the modern connoisseur relates: (Aaron Arnold opened his first store in Lower Manhattan in 1825; the company name changed to Arnold Constable & Company following the marriage of his daughter to James Mansell Constable, an employee who became a partner in 1842. In 1868 with the emergence of Union Square as retail center, the firm hired Griffith Thomas to build a new store at 881-887 Broadway. Business was brisk, and in 1876 the store was extended west along 19th Street to Fifth Avenue. This facade echoes that of the main front on Broadway except that it is executed in cast iron.)

The very materiality of the structure narrates its stratified chronology. As the architectural cognoscenti exhort with unrestrained enthusiasm: (Lovers of marble walls, cast-iron facades, and mansard roofs, rejoice! There is something here for each of you. The Broadway facade, the oldest, is of marble. The extension to Fifth Avenue, the youngest, is of cast iron, an economical simulation of its adjacent parent. In between, the 2-story miraculous mansard crown rises over the original body and the Fifth Avenue extension. It now houses publishers' offices and menswear outlets.) The commercial enterprise that animated these floors possessed a venerable pedigree, for (The business started in 1825, and by 1857 Arnold Constable was ensconced on Canal Street in a five-story building with a marble front, designed by Griffith Thomas.) The family's predilection in building materials was one of deliberate aesthetic conviction, since (Constable probably felt limestone and brick to be more imposing architecturally than the then currently popular cast iron. Note the steep two-storied mansarded roof.)

IX. THE HEROIC DIADEM OF THE MANSARD

The mansard roof invited comparison to the macabre fancies of a later imagination, for it constituted (a roof that surely could have been the inspiration for the best of cartoonist Charles Addams's designs (its only serious competition left in New York is the old Gilsey House on Broadway at Twenty-ninth Street).) The commerce transacted within its walls was of prodigious volume, the establishment being (Especially famous for its mourning clothes, it did a huge business in dry goods, carpets, and upholstery on its five selling floors. The cast-iron wing on Fifth Avenue housed the wholesale business, and the sixth and seventh floors were devoted to manufacturing.) The magnificence of the mansard was extolled anew in a parallel encomium: (With its elaborate mansard roof. Arnold Constable was one of the grandest stores on Ladies' Mile.)

The roof itself ascended to become the edifice's most beloved and conspicuous distinction. As the chronicler of cast-iron architecture affirms, (The heroic mansard roof is the feature that most often attracts attention to this big Victorian-looking cast-iron building that was once Arnold Constable's dry-goods store at the southeast corner of Fifth Avenue and 19th St. Its main facade, of white marble, was on Broadway at 19th St. The noted architect Griffith Thomas designed it in 1868.) Subsequently, (Finally, they built all the way to Fifth Avenue and erected there in 1877 an annex for the wholesale departments.) The connoisseur's catalogue located its idiom within a transatlantic aesthetic vogue, remarking of the crown that (With its bold mansard roof and richly sculptural facade, the building is an example of American architectural taste in the decade following the Civil War when designs inspired by the Paris of Napoleon III were in vogue.)

X. THE CARRIAGE TRADE AND ITS COMPETITORS

The clientele constituted the very quintessence of the age's affluence. As the chronicler of Old New York observes, (Arnold Constable & Co. (881-887 Broadway, at 19th Street) opened its "palace of trade" in 1869, and was known for the high-end fabrics and exotic carpets that would entrance the Vanderbilts and Rockefellers.) Indeed, as the venerable manual recounts, (In 1869, just about the time A. T. Stewart opened his great retail establishment at Ninth Street, Arnold, Constable & Co. moved into the large building at Broadway and Nineteenth Street, where they remained until 1915. During this long period they enjoyed a select class of trade, popularly referred to as "carriage trade.") Though rivals laid claim to a portion of this exclusive custom, (B. Altman & Co., Stern Bros, and Lord & Taylor had a considerable part of it, and kept the class of goods required for such a clientele, but Arnold, Constable & Co. had the lion's share.) The vitrines of the competitor upon the adjacent corner likewise elicited widespread admiration, for (Lord & Taylor's Broadway store in the 80s was one of the busiest and most attractive dry goods establishments in the city. They had a fine location at the corner of Twentieth Street, and made a special feature of dressing the large windows there with the latest styles in costumes and not infrequently millinery. These displays were so good that their fame spread beyond the confines of the city and people came long distances to see them.)

XI. A PERAMBULATION PAST THE PALACE OF WHITE MARBLE

To promenade past the emporium in its full efflorescence was to behold a veritable marvel, as one time-traveling narrator so evocatively recollected: (At Nineteenth we passed a magnificent store of white marble, and I glanced at a brass sign—one was set into the lower edge of each of the & co. Beside the long row of display windows-and it said ARNOLD CONSTABLE & CO.) Adjacent to the marble emporium, he descried (a middle-aged woman selling toy's from a basket sat on a tiny folding campstool next to a flight of stairs.) He passed (a man in a dark-blue army overcoat, wearing a blue forage cap—the flat Civil War kind— and he was working his way upstream of the traffic flow with a wooden trayful of apples hung from a leather sling round his neck.) He remarked upon (an elderly woman selling pressed ferns from a basket; I have no idea what they were for), and upon a mutilated veteran who (had a grind organ hanging from his neck by a strap and supported by a single leg; he was turning the handle with his one arm, cranking out—I listened to be sure, and yes, it was— "Hail, Hail, the Gang's All Here!") Overhead, the horological monuments presided, for (We were never out of sight of one or another of the great clocks set high above the crowds on elaborate iron pedestals; only the well-off carried watches, I remembered Martin's saying; they were expensive, to be passed on to sons and grandsons after them; no Timexes here.)

XII. THE CONSTABLE PATRIMONY IN MASONRY

The mercantile fortune of the house extended its dominion to the contiguous corner, for the family had apprehended the axiom that (The dry goods trade was a lucrative one for the Constable family, and they invested their profits in real estate.) Robert Maynicke, and Schickel himself.)

XIV. THE INEXORABLE MARCH TO MIDTOWN

The dissolution of the great mercantile house upon this site formed but one episode in a citywide pattern of retreat and abandonment.
